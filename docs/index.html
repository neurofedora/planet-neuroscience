    Artificial Intelligence and Imaging Technologies in Orthodontic Assessment, Referral and Care Pathways: A Scoping Review [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Abstract* Background Artificial intelligence (AI), integrated with imaging technologies, is increasingly applied across orthodontic assessment and care pathways. However, the evidence remains scattered across imaging modalities, clinical applications, AI methodologies, and stages of clinical translation. Methods A scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and reported in line with the PRISMA-ScR guidelines. PubMed, Scopus, and IEEE Xplore were searched for studies published between January 2016 and June 2026, with searches supplemented by Google Scholar, OpenAlex, grey literature sources, and citation searching. Eligible studies were mapped descriptively and synthesised thematically using Braun and Clarke’s framework. Results Fifty-four studies were included. AI applications used intraoral and facial photographs, cephalometric radiographs, CBCT, intraoral scans, three-dimensional dental models, and multimodal imaging. Applications ranged from landmark localisation, segmentation, and malocclusion classification to treatment-need assessment, screening, referral support, treatment planning, and remote monitoring. Evidence was strongest for bounded, anatomically verifiable image-analysis tasks, whereas applications involving referral, triage, treatment-need decisions, longitudinal prediction, and workflow integration were less mature. Common limitations included retrospective, single-centre designs; heterogeneous reference standards; limited external validation; inconsistent reporting of calibration and uncertainty; and insufficient evaluation of patient-centred, equity, and service-level outcomes. Conclusions Imaging-based AI in orthodontics has evolved from automated image interpretation to clinician-assisted decision support across the care pathway. Current evidence most strongly supports AI for structured image-analysis tasks, but does not yet support autonomous referral, triage, or treatment-planning decisions. Future research should prioritise prospective multicentre validation, transparent reporting, explainability, fairness, uncertainty handling, and workflow-integrated evaluation to enable safe and clinically useful implementation.

    Expression status transition of NOTCH1 accompanies chromatin remodeling in human early retinal progenitor cells [version 6; peer review: 1 approved, 3 approved with reservations]

    Background Notch signaling, especially via the Notch1 receptor, supports normal eye development by maintaining the retinal progenitor cell state before differentiation, although the regulatory mechanism behind fine-tuned Notch1 expression remains unclear. Methods We re-analyzed publicly available human fetal retina single-cell RNA/ATAC-seq data at days 59, 74, and 78, using Seurat/Signac pipelines. Results We observed NOTCH1 expression in retinal progenitor cells, which diminished later in differentiation. Integration of single-cell RNA-seq and ATAC-seq revealed decreased chromatin accessibility at the NOTCH1 locus, accompanied by decreased mRNA expression, during differentiation into bipolar cells, horizontal cells, or retinal ganglion cells. Importantly, the chromatin remodeling regions contained a binding motif for the RAX transcription factor, which is essential for retinal progenitor cells, Muller glia, and cones. This result suggested that chromatin remodeling at the NOTCH1 locus might regulate NOTCH1 expression, although single-cell RNA-seq analysis suggested that RAX expression regulation might also contribute to NOTCH1 expression. Conclusions By integrating single-cell RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data, this study identified a chromatin remodeling region containing a putative RAX-binding site in the NOTCH1 locus, suggesting that NOTCH1 expression is regulated at both the transcription factor and chromatin levels during early human retinal development.

    A trigonometric centered exploration for Fermat’s last theorem [version 2; peer review: 1 approved]

    In 1995, Andrew Wiles completed his landmark proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem, drawing upon deep machinery from algebraic geometry, modular forms, and the theory of elliptic curves, most notably the Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture. While this achievement resolved one of the most celebrated problems in mathematics, the proof’s extraordinary sophistication and reliance on twentieth-century techniques have sustained a long-standing desire within the mathematical community for a more elementary or concise demonstration. A simpler solution, one that circumvents the heavy apparatus of modern arithmetic geometry and instead rests on classical or foundational principles would not only illuminate the intrinsic structure of the theorem but also provide greater conceptual transparency. Consequently, the search for alternative, more accessible proofs remains an active and compelling intellectual pursuit. In this paper a simple, novel trigonometric approach to Fermat’s Last Theorem is presented. The Fermat’s last theorem is first reduced to a trigonometric form and then it shown that it is not possible for the trigonometric form to exist.

The suggested model applies the principles of machine learning to automatically generate prosodic analysis that extends beyond individual sound segments and reflects suprasegmental aspects of prosody including rhythm, intonation, and phrasal stress.

    Organizational and Psychological Determinants of Nursing Performance: A PLS-SEM Analysis Among Registered Nurses in Saudi Arabia [version 2; peer review: 1 not approved]

    Background Maintaining high nursing performance is increasingly challenging in modern healthcare systems due to heavy workloads, workforce shortages, and growing psychological demands. Although work-life balance has been recognized as an important organizational resource, limited evidence has examined the statistical pathways linking work-life balance with nursing performance through employee well-being and stress reduction, particularly in Saudi Arabian healthcare settings. This study examined the associations among work-life balance, employee well-being, stress reduction, and nursing performance among registered nurses. Methods A cross-sectional study was conducted among 427 registered nurses employed at The Medical City of Medina, Saudi Arabia. Proportionate stratification was used to ensure representation across clinical departments, and all eligible nurses within each stratum were invited to participate. Data were collected using structured self-administered questionnaires and analysed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM). Direct and indirect associations were evaluated using a bootstrapping procedure with 5,000 resamples. Results Work-life balance was positively associated with employee well-being (β = 0.885, p < 0.001), stress reduction (β = 0.888, p < 0.001), and nursing performance (β = 0.219, p < 0.001). Employee well-being (β = 0.320, p < 0.001) and stress reduction (β = 0.417, p < 0.001) were also positively associated with nursing performance. Statistically significant indirect associations were observed between work-life balance and nursing performance through employee well-being (β = 0.283, p < 0.001) and stress reduction (β = 0.371, p < 0.001), consistent with partial statistical mediation. Conclusions Work-life balance was positively associated with nursing performance both directly and indirectly through employee well-being and stress reduction. The findings support the relevance of organizational conditions that address work-life balance, employee well-being, and occupational stress in nursing workforce management. Given the cross-sectional design, the observed associations and statistical mediation should not be interpreted as evidence of causal relationships. Longitudinal and multi-centre research is warranted to further examine these relationships across diverse healthcare settings.

    The Reflection of Cryptocurrencies Prices Volatility on US Financial Markets as a Factor in Investment Portfolio Optimization: An Experimental Study in The American Financial Markets [version 3; peer review: 1 approved with reservations, 1 not approved]

    This study investigates theoretical frameworks defining the relationship between cryptocurrency volatility and the performance of financial market indices. Specifically, it empirically examines the impact of major cryptocurrencies namely BNB, Bitcoin, Tether, Ethereum and XRP on selected US stock market indices, specifically the S&P 500, NASDAQ Composite, and Dow Jones Industrial Average, over the monthly period from 2020 to 2024 (yielding 60 monthly observations). Utilizing the Autoregressive Distributed Lag (ARDL) bounds testing approach, the empirical methodology is selected due to its robustness in capturing both short- and long-run cointegrating relationships among variables, effectively accommodating mixed orders of integration while addressing potential endogeneity issues. The empirical results indicate that Bitcoin and Tether exert a statistically significant positive effect on the performance of the examined US stock market indices over the long term at the 5% significance level (p < 0.05). Conversely, Ethereum demonstrates a statistically significant negative relationship with these indices at the same significance level (p < 0.05). While this inverse relationship suggests that Ethereum may exhibit potential diversification benefits, classifying it strictly as a "hedge" or "safe-haven" asset requires rigorous high-frequency stress-testing during periods of extreme market downturns; thus, such assertions are tempered in light of the current monthly findings. From a policy and portfolio management perspective, the findings suggest that investors might consider incorporating Bitcoin and Tether alongside traditional asset classes, provided that appropriate risk-management frameworks are applied. Furthermore, regulatory authorities are encouraged to develop clear and adaptive compliance guidelines to accommodate the integration of digital assets within institutional investment portfolios.

    Using the aperiodic slope across learning to predict sleep-based memory consolidation

    Homeostatic mechanisms adjust synaptic strengths to regulate neuronal activity across wake-sleep cycles. Although sleep supports memory consolidation, limited research has explored how sleep-induced homeostatic changes influence memory outcomes in humans. The EEG-derived aperiodic slope (1/f) could be a candidate measure of homeostatic memory processes through its reflection of neural excitation-inhibition balance. In this study, 27 participants learned object-word pairs, and completed an immediate recognition test before either a nap or equivalent wakeful period. Resting-state EEG was recorded, both before and after learning and the interval, to derive changes in aperiodic slope values. A delayed recognition test then assessed whether changes in the aperiodic slope (i.e., homeostatic activity) across learning and the interval predicted memory accuracy and N400 amplitude, an index of stimulus expectancy and recognition. Our study found evidence to suggest homeostatic renormalisation, for the nap condition only, indicated by a shift towards inhibition across sleep which was inversely related to the slope change across learning. Although the aperiodic slope change across the nap and wake intervals did not predict delayed memory accuracy, a shift towards inhibition during learning was beneficial for memory accuracy, only in the nap condition. Similarly, a shift towards inhibition across learning, followed by a shift towards excitation in the nap condition, predicted reduced N400 amplitudes. This suggests that homeostatic renormalisation across the nap influenced model updating and stimulus expectancy. This study provides evidence for a link between synaptic homeostatic processes and memory consolidation, emphasising the importance of encoding-related aperiodic activity in this relationship.

    Identifying Network Factors Driving Changes in Brain Connectivity During Aging via Stochastic Actor-Oriented Models

    Brain network organization emerges from ongoing network dynamics that continuously create, maintain, and reorganize connectivity patterns. At both the structural and functional levels, these dynamics shape network evolution throughout the lifespan. Here, we introduce a data-driven framework based on Stochastic Actor-Oriented Models (SAOMs), which model longitudinal network changes as a process of local edge formation and dissolution driven by local-connectivity factors. Applied to functional and structural brain networks from an aging cohort, the resulting models recovered a set of interacting mechanisms that collectively promote hub formation, short-range connectivity, and modular organization. These factors were derived through a novel data-driven factor-selection procedure rather than specified a priori, allowing network mechanisms underlying longitudinal change to be identified directly from observations. Functional networks exhibited substantially higher rates of change than structural networks, suggesting more frequent opportunities for network maintenance and reorganization. Structural networks additionally showed despecification in the hub-forming mechanisms with cognitive impairment. These results support a view of brain network organization as an actively maintained process emerging from ongoing local connectivity dynamics. More broadly, this framework provides a general approach for studying longitudinal network dynamics through the estimation of network-generating mechanisms over time.

    Inferotemporal cortex stimulation is perceived before its onset

    Neural response latency measures when stimulus-evoked activity becomes detectable, but it does not reveal when the brain assigns the causal contribution of that activity to events in the external world. We define this temporal adjustment as projected delay and measure it in the macaque inferotemporal cortex using optogenetic stimulation and behavior. Two rhesus macaques performed a cortical perturbation detection task in which a visual masker appeared at different times relative to stimulation. Detection was selectively impaired when the masker began 200 ms before stimulation. We then tested this timing using Perceptography, a method that optimizes image perturbations to mimic stimulation-evoked visual events. Image perturbations presented 200 ms before stimulation elicited higher false-alarm rates than perturbations presented at stimulation onset. Here, we show that stimulation-evoked visual events in inferotemporal cortex depend preferentially on visual information originating approximately 200 ms before the cortical perturbation. This projected delay exceeds typical inferotemporal response latencies and suggests temporally extended readout mechanisms relevant to causal models of visual perception and the timing of cortical visual prostheses.

    A Computational Model Captures Orexin-Dependent Modulation of Memory Consolidation.

    Hippocampal sharp-wave ripple (SWR) events occur during post-experience rest and sleep and play a significant role in memory consolidation. Recent ex vivo studies have found orexin-driven neuromodulatory effects on SWR parameters and proposed that they can influence memory consolidation. Additionally, behavioral studies demonstrate contradictory results regarding orexinergic effects on memory acquisition depending on task structure and intervention time. This inconsistency, along with the diffuse spread of orexin receptors (OXR) in the CA1 and CA3 hippocampus, challenges theoretical interpretation of orexinergic effects on memory. To reconcile this problem, we propose a rate-based model linking orexinergic tone-dependent SWR statistics to memory performance. The model combines an encoding module whose gain scales with the global orexinergic tone with a consolidation module driven by OX2R-specific replay dynamics. The model is based on a plasticity rule that considers SWR events as the unit of induction. Therefore, weight change requires sufficient replay content and depolarizing drive. We observed that, depending on the task structure, encoding-dominant learning benefits discrimination as the orexin drive increases. Consolidation-dominant learning shows a non-monotonic relationship with an internal optimum level of orexinergic tone. This interaction highlights that stronger truncation at higher receptor engagement shortens memory replay and reduces the efficacy of the consolidation process. The model predicts that the optimal regime depends on task complexity reflected in sequence length. Together, these results demonstrate how task demands determine whether increased orexinergic drive enhances or impairs effective consolidation and SWR-dependent replay in the hippocampus.

    A Bayesian approach to volumetric analysis in rare neurodegenerative disorders

    Identifying regional brain atrophy is critical for understanding neurodegenerative disorders. Traditionally, group comparisons rely on frequentist statistics, utilising $p$-values and point estimates that offer limited characterization of uncertainty. Bayesian methods provide an alternative by estimating effect sizes and credible intervals while incorporating prior knowledge and uncertainty. This study evaluated the feasibility of Bayesian hierarchical modelling to assess regional brain atrophy in Progressive Supranuclear Palsy (PSP) and Corticobasal Syndrome (CBS) compared to healthy controls. Participants meeting diagnostic criteria for CBS or PSP, together with healthy controls, were recruited from the Cambridge Centre for Parkinson-plus. Grey matter volume, cortical thickness, and subcortical volumes were derived using FreeSurfer for 210 cortical and 36 subcortical regions defined by the Human Brainnetome Atlas. Participants with PSP were classified into Richardson syndrome (PSP-RS), corticobasal syndrome variant (PSP-CBS), frontal variant (PSP-F), and other presentations (PSP-Other). Bayesian hierarchical ANOVA models with appropriate priors were used to estimate group differences. Regions of practical equivalence (ROPE) were calculated to assess evidence of meaningful group effects. A total of 428 people were included (113 PSP-RS, 30 PSP-CBS, 23 PSP-Other, 19 PSP-F, 81 CBS, and 162 healthy controls). For grey matter volume, there was evidence of whole brain volume loss compared to controls for all disease groups except PSP-Other, which showed evidence of no difference. For cortical thickness, there was evidence of global cortical thinning in CBS compared to controls, but for no other group. For subcortical volume all groups showed evidence of volume loss compared to controls. Patterns of regional atrophy were identified in keeping with previous literature, with the largest effect observed in the left pre-motor thalamus (30% reduction in CBS). There was greater loss of subcortical volume in PSP-RS compared to other PSP subgroups. Bayesian hierarchical modelling provides a feasible framework for quantifying regional brain atrophy, with interpretable effect size estimates and uncertainty measures. This approach complements conventional statistical methods, particularly in the assessment of rare neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric disorders.

In addition, immunohistochemical analyses of post mortem brain tissue from patients with dementia with Lewy bodies demonstrated ASC expression in regions exhibiting Syn pathology and showed ASC co-localization with Syn within Lewy bodies. Collectively, these findings identify ASC-Syn assemblies as potent amplifiers of NLRP3 inflammasome signaling and suggest a mechanism through which they may contribute to sustained neuroinflammation in synucleinopathies.

    Brief Visual Deprivation Selectively Remodels Intracortical Inputs onto Layer 6 Corticothalamic Neurons in Primary Auditory Cortex

    Loss of one sensory modality can enhance processing in spared systems. Brief visual deprivation by dark exposure (DE) enhances auditory responses in the primary auditory cortex (A1) of adult mice, increasing firing rates, frequency selectivity, and behavioral performance. This enhancement is accompanied by reduced inhibition of the auditory thalamus (MGBv) and strengthened thalamocortical and intracortical feedforward inputs. While the mechanisms coordinating cross-modal cortical and thalamic plasticity are unknown, Ntsr1+ layer 6 corticothalamic neurons (L6-CTNs) are a promising candidate given their regulation of both cortical and thalamic gain. Here, we examined how 1 week of DE in both male and female adult mice alters intracortical synaptic inputs onto A1 L6-CTNs using laser-scanning photostimulation and whole-cell recordings. We found DE selectively reduced excitatory intracortical connectivity onto L6-CTNs across multiple layers and weakened synaptic strength from infragranular inputs. In contrast, inhibitory inputs were largely preserved. This shift resulted in a layer-specific reduction in excitation–inhibition balance, particularly for inputs from layer 4. Although the spatial extent of inputs was unchanged, DE decreased the within-animal similarity of excitatory input patterns across neurons, indicating a reorganization of circuit structure. Together, these findings identify selective weakening of intracortical excitation onto L6-CTNs as a key feature of cross-modal plasticity. By reducing recruitment of the L6 gain-control circuit, this reconfiguration may bias cortical processing toward enhanced sensory discrimination while preserving sensitivity.

    ABO Blood Group and HIV Infection in Sub-Saharan Africa: Disentangling Biological Signal from Population-Level Confounding [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Background Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) bears the world’s greatest HIV burden, prompting sustained interest in host genetic modifiers of susceptibility, including the ABO blood group system. Yet reported associations between ABO phenotype and HIV infection remain inconsistent, and whether they reflect true biological effects or uncontrolled population structure is unresolved. Methods We conducted a PRISMA-informed structured narrative review of studies examining ABO phenotype and HIV infection in SSA, searched across PubMed/MEDLINE, Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and African Journals Online. Given marked heterogeneity in design, denominators, and reporting across eligible studies, a formal meta-analysis was not undertaken. Instead, for studies reporting extractable population-level ABO frequencies, we explicitly tested whether HIV-associated phenotype patterns were distinguishable from background distribution. Results Fifteen studies from nine SSA countries met inclusion criteria. Blood group O was the most prevalent phenotype both in the general population and among HIV-positive individuals in every study reporting background data. Among six studies with quantifiable denominators, only one (Korir et al., Kenya) used an independent background comparator; its HIV-positive proportion (45.6%, 95% CI 39.8–51.4%) fell squarely within the background range (45–49%), showing no detectable excess. The remaining five studies derived HIV and background estimates from the same sample, precluding independent confounding assessment. A notable outlier — 46.2% blood group AB among an HIV-exposed infant cohort (Okonkwo et al.) versus an expected 3–5% — represented the largest deviation observed, though based on a small subgroup requiring replication. Only two of fifteen studies reported adjusted estimates. Conclusion Existing SSA evidence does not support a reproducible, confounding-independent association between ABO phenotype and HIV infection; apparent group-O associations largely reflect background prevalence. Standardized reporting of adjusted estimates and population phenotype distributions is needed going forward.
