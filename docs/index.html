    Alignment of visual brain encoding models with individual perceptual similarity judgments

    arXiv:2610.22569v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Encoding models that predict brain activation responses to images have traditionally been trained to maximize accuracy when predicting observed brain data. However, their internal representations may not reflect individual human perception. In this work, we investigate whether we can fine-tune brain encoding models to better align their representations with individuals' similarity judgments. Using similarity judgments of 100 images from 8 participants in the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD), we adapt a generic human similarity judgment model (DreamSim) separately for each participant to obtain personalized perceptual similarity models. These models are then used to obtain subject-specific similarity structure across images in their encoding model training sets. We fine-tune each participant's baseline encoder in two conditions, aligning its representations with either generic or personalized similarity judgments, with the same initialization and objective. We find that baseline brain encoders reflect within-subject perceptual similarity more strongly than measured fMRI responses and preserve intersubject correlations present in measured fMRI, but fail to reflect those of human perceptual similarity judgments. Fine-tuning with generic, population-level perceptual similarity information results in better alignment with human judgments than baseline encoders, but these models still fail to capture intersubject correlations of human judgments. However, fine-tuning with personalized perceptual similarity information results in models that are the most aligned with human perceptual similarity judgments and also reflect intersubject correlations of human judgments. Our framework provides an approach for bootstrapping human similarity judgment data into more individualized, human-aligned brain encoding models of vision.

    EEG Lempel-Ziv Complexity vis-\`a-vis Autocorrelation Function

    arXiv:2610.23231v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Measures of signal complexity have become increasingly popular in electroencephalography (EEG), where they are often interpreted as indicators of neural information processing, dynamical richness, or conscious state. In contrast, classical measures of temporal dependence, such as the autocorrelation function (ACF), remain more directly linked to known physiological mechanisms and are easier to interpret. The present work revisits, for the EEG practicioner, the relation between normalized Lempel-Ziv complexity (LZC) and temporal autocorrelation in both synthetic and physiological EEG signals. First, synthetic time series exhibiting a broad range of temporal structure and complexity levels were generated using stochastic processes with varying degrees of temporal dependence. LZC and autocorrelation measures were then computed and compared. Across simulated signals, a robust inverse relationship was observed between normalized LZC and the first autocorrelation coefficient, indicating that signals with stronger temporal correlations exhibit lower complexity estimates. This relationship also holds in resting-state EEG recordings from healthy adult participants across channels and subjects, as well as dynamically during eyes open/close maneuvers. Overall, these results show that the information captured by LZC reflects temporal redundancy already quantified by conventional autocorrelation measures. Because autocorrelation measures relate more directly to established neurophysiological mechanisms, including oscillatory persistence, network synchronization, and temporal integration, these results suggest that interpreting complexity-based EEG findings should explicitly account for their dependence on conventional temporal statistics, which may provide a more transparent framework for mechanistic inferences.

    Distributional signatures of computation: inferring mechanisms from computation time distributions

    arXiv:2610.23607v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Computational complexity theory provides a framework for analysing abstract models of computation, whereas computational systems operate as finite physical implementations whose behaviour is shaped by their architecture and physical substrate. In such systems, computational organisation cannot be directly characterised from algorithms or internal representations. Building on the use of reaction time distributions to constrain mechanisms in cognitive science, we introduce an empirical framework for investigating computation in physical systems by inferring computational mechanisms from observable computation time distributions. The framework combines empirical benchmarking, which characterises computational efficiency through the scaling of computation time with problem size, with distributional analysis, which treats computation time distributions as signatures of the stochastic dynamics generated by different computational mechanisms. We demonstrate the framework using a scalable visual search paradigm, comparing human performance with three computational models that employ distinct mechanisms of evidence accumulation. Benchmarking reveals broadly similar scaling behaviour for the models and human observers, whereas distributional analysis distinguishes between these models. We show that computation time distributions provide an empirical level of description linking observable computational behaviour to underlying computational mechanisms. This framework complements computational complexity theory by extending the empirical study of computation from measuring computational performance to characterising computational organisation. It provides an empirical approach for comparing finite computational systems across diverse physical implementations, including biological, classical, neuromorphic, and quantum systems, without requiring access to their internal algorithms or architectures.

    Brain-Aligned Multimodal LLMs for Social and Emotional Understanding of Cartoon Movies

    arXiv:2610.22706v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) offer scalable annotations of social and affective content, but their temporal agreement with human judgments and neural predictive value require further evaluation. We compared diffusion-based and autoregressive MLLMs with continuous human Theory of Mind (ToM) and affective valence ratings across three short cartoon movies. Behavioral analyses assessed temporally aligned Pearson correlations, rating distributions, and the effects of preceding scene descriptions. Ratings from each source were separately used in subject-specific regression models to predict regional fMRI responses. Model ratings captured aspects of human temporal variation despite systematic distributional differences, with generally stronger alignment for valence than ToM. Preceding context improved alignment in some conditions, depending on the model and movie. Spatial overlap across rating sources occurred mainly among MLLMs; overlap between human and model ratings was limited and involved primarily visual, dorsal-attention, and posterior default-mode regions. These findings support MLLMs as scalable tools for generating brain-predictive annotations of social and emotional content, while highlighting systematic rating differences and model- and stimulus-dependent alignment with human judgments.

    Deep LLM layers expose passage-level context in EEG during silent reading

    arXiv:2610.23638v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: In a previous report we showed that a decoder can recover open-vocabulary word-level information from 19-channel EEG recorded during silent reading, by aligning short EEG windows with hidden-state embeddings of the presented word taken from a large language model (LLM). That analysis contrasted two of the model's 33 layers: the context-free input embedding and one mid-depth layer. Here we ask how EEG-decodable structure is distributed over the full depth of the language model. We trained one decoder per layer of Llama-3.1-8B, with and without a causal transformer over the sequence of EEG windows. We distinguish word-level decodability from a context-tracking component. Word-level decodability peaks in early layers and declines steadily towards the last layer. In contrast, the context-tracking component of the retrieval gain rises with depth, both with and without a causal transformer. Unexpectedly, a decoder that sees a single 0.5-s window at a time tracks the text passage too. Two new controls dissect this component. A target-geometry control, a synthetic predictor made of the true target plus noise calibrated to each decoder's word-level accuracy, shows that context tracking is not a by-product of word decoding and target geometry. A control that permutes predictions across runs from either the same or from different sessions shows that most of the observed context-tracking is not caused by an EEG recording session signature. Instead, context tracking mostly follows the text. A single EEG window therefore carries passage-level information that a contextual target can absorb. The results locate word identity in the early layers of the language model, discourse state in the deeper layers, and make the choice of target layer a design dial for EEG language decoders.

    Effect of an auditory static distractor on the perception of an auditory moving target

    arXiv:2510.25119v4 Announce Type: replace Abstract: It is known that listeners lose the ability to discriminate the direction of motion of a revolving sound (clockwise vs counterclockwise) beyond a critical velocity ["the upper limit (UL)"], likely due to degraded front-back discrimination. Little is known about how this ability is affected by simultaneously present distractor sounds, despite the real-life importance of tracking moving sounds in the presence of distractors. We hypothesized that the presence of a static distractor sound would impair the perception of moving target sounds and reduce the UL and show that this is indeed the case. A distractor on the right was as effective as a distractor at the front in reducing the UL, despite the likely importance of resolving front-back confusions. By manipulating the spectral content of both the target and distractor, we found that the UL was significantly reduced only when the distractor spectrally overlaps with the target in the frequency range relevant for front-back discrimination. Our findings form the first steps toward a better understanding of the tracking of multiple sounds in the presence of distractors.

    Inter- and Intra-Subject Variability in EEG: A Systematic Survey

    arXiv:2602.01019v2 Announce Type: replace Abstract: Electroencephalography (EEG) underpins neuroscience, clinical neurophysiology, and brain-computer interfaces (BCIs), yet pronounced inter- and intra-subject variability limits reliability, reproducibility, and translation. This systematic review studies that quantified or modeled EEG variability across resting-state, event-related potentials (ERPs), and task-related/BCI paradigms (including motor imagery and SSVEP) in healthy and clinical cohorts. Across paradigms, inter-subject differences are typically larger than within-subject fluctuations, but both affect inference and model generalization. Stability is feature-dependent: alpha-band measures and individual alpha peak frequency are often relatively reliable, whereas higher-frequency and many connectivity-derived metrics show more heterogeneous reliability; ERP reliability varies by component, with P300 measures frequently showing moderate-to-good stability. We summarize major sources of variability (biological, state-related, technical, and analytical), review common quantification and modeling approaches (e.g., ICC, CV, SNR, generalizability theory, and multivariate/learning-based methods), and provide recommendations for study design, reporting, and harmonization. Overall, EEG variability should be treated as both a practical constraint to manage and a meaningful signal to leverage for precision neuroscience and robust neurotechnology.

    Learning reveals invisible structure in low-rank RNNs

    arXiv:2605.04115v2 Announce Type: replace-cross Abstract: Learning in neural systems arises from synaptic changes that reshape the representations underlying behavior. While low-rank recurrent neural networks (RNNs) have emerged as a powerful framework for linking connectivity to function, a theoretical understanding of their learning process remains elusive. Here, we extend the low-rank framework from activity to learning by deriving gradient-descent dynamics directly in a reduced overlap space. We formulate a closed-form, low-dimensional system of ODEs that governs learning in this space, exact for linear RNNs and asymptotically exact for nonlinear RNNs in the large-$N$ Gaussian limit. Central to our analysis is a distinction between two classes of overlaps: loss-visible overlaps, which fully determine network activity, output, and loss, and loss-invisible overlaps, which do not affect function but are required to describe learning. We illustrate the consequences of this decomposition through two phenomena. First, we show that learning can serve as a perturbation that exposes differences in connectivity between functionally equivalent networks. Second, we show that loss-invisible overlaps can act as memory variables that encode training history, and characterize the conditions under which this occurs. Finally, we present several testable predictions for biological learning experiments derived from our theory.

    Upper bounds on the number of causal pieces in spiking neural networks

    arXiv:2610.22484v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: The input space of single-spike neural networks decomposes into causal pieces, and the number of causal pieces has been shown to provide a lower bound on the approximation error for some spiking neural networks. While this decomposition is applicable to a wide range of spiking neuron models, the limits of how many causal pieces can be realised by spiking neural networks are still unknown. In this work, we derive two complementary upper bounds on the number of causal pieces in feedforward single-spike neural networks. First, we establish a combinatorial bound given by the poly-Bernoulli numbers, which applies to a broad class of spiking neuron models. The bound is of order $e^{2(L-1)N\log N+\mathcal{O}(N)}$, with network depth $L$ and maximum layer width $N$. Second, for a specific set of neuron models, we derive a bound based on hyperplane arrangements. Applying the latter framework, we obtain bounds of order $\mathcal{O}\!\left(N^{(4L-3)(N_0-1)}\right)$ for the non-leaky integrate-and-fire model with exponential synapses and the ReLU-type spike response model, and $\mathcal{O}\!\left(N^{L(N_0-1)}\right)$ for the leaky integrate-and-fire model with Dirac-$\delta$ synapses, where $N_0$ is the input dimension. For the non-leaky integrate-and-fire model with Dirac-$\delta$ synapses, additional temporal structure allows us to sharpen the general combinatorial bound to one that depends only on $N_0$, independently of the network depth and widths.

    From anatomy to computation: axonal delay dispersion shifts neurons from event to sequence detection

    arXiv:2609.04195v3 Announce Type: replace-cross Abstract: Cortical neurons fire sparsely -- often fewer than one spike per sensory window -- so a neuron's spike count in that window carries little information, and its spike timing can carry more. That conduction delays convert firing order into synchrony is long established. What governs which class of temporal feature a neuron detects -- one volley of coincident input, or two in a particular order -- has not been examined. We propose a delay-signature framework in which the axonal conduction delays converging on a dendritic branch or a small neuron's soma constitute a physical key: only input sequences whose spike-time differences the delays compensate arrive synchronously, and coincidence detection, a threshold on input summed within an inhibition-compressed window, converts that synchrony into an all-or-none output. First, in simulations of an integrator-neuron model, a single physical scalar -- the dispersion of the delay set -- moves a population from event to order-selective sequence detection. The transition is emergent under random delays and connectivity, and an exact calculation reproduces it: at narrow dispersion, sequence detectors do not exist, and the dispersion at which they overtake event detectors tracks the inter-event interval. This maps a computational distinction onto the anatomical one between myelinated and unmyelinated projections, making myelination a switch on what a neuron computes. Second, the same dispersion sets the code's limits: it bounds the longest codable interval and fixes an absolute timing tolerance of order a millisecond, the same at every dispersion. Third, that millisecond window and the conduction velocity of afferents inside cortex together set column diameter, and column anatomy predicts the window measured in mouse barrel cortex. One anatomically measurable parameter thus shapes what a neuron detects and sets the limits of what it can represent.

    SpikeQ: Spike-Guided Post-Training Quantization for Spiking Neural Networks

    arXiv:2610.23046v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: On neuromorphic chips, spiking neural networks (SNNs) consume far less energy than artificial neural networks (ANNs), but the limited memory of these chips requires low-bit synaptic weights and membrane potentials. Existing post-training quantization (PTQ) methods provide them without retraining and, like those for ANNs, minimize numerical errors such as the mean squared error (MSE). In an SNN, however, a quantization error reaches later layers only through the spikes it flips. We find that errors of equal MSE can cause very different accuracy losses, whereas errors that flip the same fraction of spikes cause the same loss. We therefore propose SpikeQ, a spike-guided PTQ framework. For weights, we select the level of each weight by simulating the downstream neurons and minimizing their spike errors. For membrane potentials, most existing methods quantize the state after the reset, whereas neuromorphic chips compare the threshold with the membrane potential stored at a fixed bit width. We target this pre-fire quantization and build a reset-aware grid that places the threshold on a rounding boundary, so rounding never flips the spike at the current time step. We evaluate SpikeQ on seven benchmarks of image classification, semantic segmentation, and object detection. With 2-bit pre-fire membrane potentials, every existing grid collapses, whereas SpikeQ retains most of the full-precision accuracy. With both at 4 bits, the models stay within 2.59 points of full precision, shrink their memory footprint 7.8--7.9 times, and use 6.4--6.9 times less energy.

    Wavelength Diversity Expands the Expressive Power of Optical Computing

    arXiv:2610.23070v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: Universal function approximation underpins neural networks' capacity to represent nonlinear relationships, but realizing this capability in optical hardware requires engineering the computational features encoded in light. Here, we show that wavelength diversity enhances optical function-approximation expressivity through wavelength-scaled Fourier features generated by a shared phase encoder, without increasing the number of input-encoded harmonics. Multiple wavelengths contribute to the representation of each output function rather than merely serving as independent computational channels. We establish theoretical scaling laws and approximation-error bounds, revealing that, for nondispersive encoders, the spectral endpoints determine the accessible computational bandwidth, while intermediate illumination wavelengths enrich the available harmonics within that bandwidth. Numerical studies demonstrate improved approximation of real- and complex-valued nonlinear functions, with a representative design achieving more than a 100-fold reduction in mean-squared error relative to tested monochromatic optical processors with the same number of trainable phase features. Experimentally, we validate enhanced approximation accuracy and demonstrate the simultaneous approximation of 4,136 distinct nonlinear functions using a compact, single-layer diffractive processor under five-wavelength illumination. These results establish wavelength diversity as an additional physical resource for scaling the expressive power of optical computing and enhancing the accuracy of parallel nonlinear function approximation.

    Riding the Fixed-Point Curve: Image Classification in the Continuous Thought Machine

    arXiv:2610.19220v2 Announce Type: replace-cross Abstract: The Continuous Thought Machine (CTM) is a recurrent network that attends to inputs and makes predictions based on the synchronization between its neurons.     Computation-through-Dynamics Toolkit: Simulated datasets and quality metrics for dynamical models of neural activity

    A primary goal of systems neuroscience is to discover how ensembles of neurons transform inputs into goal-directed behavior, a process known as neural computation. A powerful framework for understanding neural computation uses neural dynamics – the rules that govern how neural activity evolves over time – to explain how goal-directed input-output transformations occur. As dynamical rules are not directly observable, we need computational models that can infer neural dynamics from recorded neural activity. In summary, CtDToolkit provides a necessary framework for model developers to better understand and characterize neural computation through the lens of dynamics.
