    Techno-Economic and Security-Constrained Expansion Planning of the 132-kV Western Uganda Transmission Network [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Background Rapid demand growth can expose developing transmission systems to corridor overload, voltage deterioration and contingency vulnerability before major reinforcement is completed. Methods We evaluated expansion of the 132-kV Western Uganda network using full AC power flow, multi-objective planning, N−1 contingency analysis, demand-growth stress testing and discounted economic assessment. Results At 184 MW and 75 MVAr base demand, the existing network lost 2.8767 MW and the Mutundwe–Kabulasoke corridor reached 131.67% loading. A two-circuit plan—Mbarara North–Masaka West and an additional Mutundwe–Kabulasoke circuit—costing USD 117.76 million reduced losses to 1.7261 MW, aggregate voltage deviation from 0.1908 to 0.0974 p.u., and maximum loading to 64.66%. Secure N−1 outcomes improved from 0/12 to 11/14, but two radial-islanding risks and one bulk-entry overload remained. The expanded network met voltage and thermal limits through 120% demand; thermal loading reached 111.7% at 130%. Loss-only discounted benefits produced negative net present value across the tested assumptions. Conclusions The results indicate that transmission reinforcement can improve technical performance and contingency security, while a complete investment appraisal also requires monetised reliability, resilience and transfer-capacity benefits.

    Sketch of a Novel Approach to a Neural Model [version 3; peer review: 1 approved with reservations, 2 not approved]

    There is room on the inside. We present an account of neuroplasticity with respect to cell-internal processing pathways and their relation to membrane and synaptic plasticity. We think traditional synapse-centric, weight-based models of memorization are not sufficient or adequate to capture the complexity of neuroplasticity. In standard accounts, we model a network of neurons connected by adaptive transmission links. The adaptation of these transmission links is overly simplified using short-term and long-term potentiation/depression, assuming weight changes according to use of the transmission link. In contrast, we propose a paradigm switch from a synapse-centric model (each synapse learns independently, based on its history of use) to a neuron-centric model (each neuron uses signal selection for intracellular pathways to express plasticity at the membrane). Each neuron has a ‘vertical’ dimension where internal parameters steer the external membrane- and synapse-expressed parameters. A neural model consists of (a) expression of parameters at the membrane, in particular dendritic synapses or spines, and axonal boutons (b) internal parameters in the sub-membrane zone and the cytoplasm with its protein signaling network and (c) core parameters in the nucleus for genetic and epigenetic information. In a neuron-centric model, each node (=neuron) in the horizontal network has its own internal memory. Neural transmission and information storage are separated, not automatically combined by coupling strength. There is filtering and selection of signals for storage. Not every transmission event leaves a trace. This represents an important conceptual advance over synaptic weight models. We present the neuron as a self-programming device, rather than as passively determined by ongoing input. We believe a new approach to neural modeling is necessary, because the experimental evidence is not well captured by traditional synapse-centric models. Ultimately, we are interested in the possibilities of a flexible memory system that processes external signals according to its inherent structure.

    Teacher-Mediated, Not Learner-Facing: A Bayesian Quasi-Experimental Study of Agentic AI Support in Grade 10 Functions and Graphs [version 4; peer review: 2 approved with reservations]

    Background Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being integrated into school mathematics, yet most research has focused on learner-facing systems rather than AI that supports teachers during instruction. This study examined whether teacher-mediated agentic AI could improve Grade 10 learners’ achievement in functions and graphs while preserving teachers’ central instructional role. Methods A quasi-experimental study was conducted with 115 Grade 10 learners from two intact classes following the same eight-week instructional programme. One class received teacher-mediated AI-supported instruction, while the comparison class received conventional instruction. Bayesian baseline-adjusted models estimated differences in post-test achievement, mastery-threshold probabilities, and variation across baseline achievement levels. Learners’ mathematical interest and confidence were analysed as secondary outcomes. Results The Bayesian model estimated a baseline-adjusted advantage of 29.12 points for the AI-supported condition (95% credible interval: 27.31–30.92). Posterior estimates indicated substantially higher probabilities of reaching achievement thresholds of 70, 80, 90, and 95 under teacher-mediated AI-supported instruction than under conventional instruction. The estimated achievement contrast remained positive across the observed baseline achievement distribution. Learners in the AI-supported condition also demonstrated higher post-intervention interest and confidence than those receiving conventional instruction. Because the study employed two intact classes taught by different teachers, the findings should be interpreted cautiously as evidence from a quasi-experimental design rather than definitive causal effects. Conclusions Teacher-mediated agentic AI represents a distinct instructional configuration in which AI extends teachers’ pedagogical capacity without replacing professional judgement. Although the findings suggest considerable potential for supporting mathematics teaching, larger multi-teacher studies are needed to determine the robustness and generalisability of these effects.

    Housing Insecurity and Housing Governance in Australia: Rethinking SDG 11 and Urban Inequality [version 2; peer review: 1 approved with reservations, 1 not approved]

    Housing insecurity is an increasing concern in both developed and developing countries. In Australia, housing-cost stress, insecure tenure, inadequate housing, overcrowding and homelessness coexist with high levels of national prosperity, raising questions about housing governance and the capacity of Sustainable Development Goal 11 (SDG 11) to capture inequalities within affluent societies. This study examines housing insecurity in Australia through the interconnected lenses of housing governance, urban inequality and sustainable development. It adopts a qualitative perspective-paper design combining narrative evidence synthesis, critical policy analysis and descriptive analysis of publicly available secondary data from Australian government institutions. The findings demonstrate that housing insecurity extends beyond homelessness and varies by income, tenure, sex, age, First Nations status and geographical location. Although the estimated number of people experiencing homelessness increased from 89,733 in 2006 to 122,494 in 2021, the population-adjusted rate rose from 45 per 10,000 in 2006 to 50 in 2016 before declining to 48 in 2021. Trends also differed across homelessness categories and population groups, with the female rate increasing between 2016 and 2021 while the male rate declined. Young adults and First Nations peoples continued to experience rates substantially above the national average. The study concludes that SDG 11 is necessary but insufficient as a standalone framework for understanding housing insecurity. Effective responses require disaggregated and multidimensional monitoring alongside coordinated housing, income-support, health, family-violence, disability, employment and homelessness-prevention policies.

    A Region-Specific Dataset of Pressure Injury Photographs for Automated Staging and Clinical AI Applications [version 2; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Background Accurate staging of pressure injuries is critical for clinical management but remains challenging because of inter-observer variability. Existing datasets often lack diversity in skin tones, limiting the generalizability of artificial intelligence (AI) models. Methods This Data Note describes a curated dataset of 267 clinically labeled pressure injury images collected from hospitalized patients in southeastern Iran between May 2023 and April 2024. Images were captured using standard mobile phone cameras under variable lighting, angle, and distance to reflect real-world clinical settings. Each image was labeled by a panel of three wound care specialists according to the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP) staging system, covering Stages 1 to 4. Conclusions This dataset is intended to support the development and validation of deep learning models for automated pressure injury staging, particularly for populations with olive skin tones that are underrepresented in current literature. The dataset is available from Figshare under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International license.

    Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation modulates auditory-evoked responses and conflict adaptation in humans

    arXiv:2611.20920v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) engages ascending neuromodulatory systems, and animal studies have motivated the hypothesis that it shifts sensory cortical processing toward feedforward (FF) relative to feedback (FB) transmission. Whether transcutaneous auricular VNS (taVNS) produces a corresponding pattern in humans remains unclear. In a single-blind, sham-controlled crossover study, 11 healthy adults received 120 min of taVNS (25 Hz, left tragus) and sham stimulation (left earlobe) in separate sessions. Click-evoked EEG was recorded before, twice during, and immediately after stimulation. Sensory discrimination thresholds and conflict adaptation in a Simon task were assessed before and during stimulation. Relative to Sham, taVNS attenuated the decline in the early P0 response across the session and enhanced the Pa-Nb response early during stimulation. The later P2 response did not show the increase observed under Sham. After stimulation, taVNS reduced click-locked theta (5-8 Hz) inter-trial phase coherence relative to Sham, with no significant between-condition difference in the change in theta power. Conflict adaptation increased under taVNS, whereas sensory thresholds and the overall Simon effect did not differ significantly between conditions. In exploratory analysis, greater decreases in theta phase coherence were associated with greater increases in conflict adaptation. These findings indicate that taVNS modulates auditory responses across processing stages and conflict-related behavior. The pattern is compatible with altered FF/FB weighting, but the direction of information flow and the underlying neuromodulatory mechanisms were not measured directly.

    Midsagittal Corpus Callosum Parameterization: A Framework for Spatially Detailed Diffusion MR Imaging Analysis

    arXiv:2611.19066v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: The corpus callosum (CC) is the largest white matter structure in the human brain and plays a central role in interhemispheric communication. Diffusion MR imaging is widely used to investigate microstructural organization, but commonly used analysis approaches have important limitations. Voxel-based methods rely heavily on accurate inter-subject registration and smoothing choices, tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) restricts the analysis to a narrow white matter skeleton, and parcellation approaches reduce spatial detail by averaging diffusion measures over relatively large regions. In this work, we introduce a parameterization framework for the analysis of the midsagittal CC that resamples the structure using a standardized grid of points, establishing a geometric correspondence across individuals without explicit inter-subject registration. In a test-retest dataset, the method provided stable measurements while covering most of the midsagittal CC, and sensitivity analyses showed that results in the central CC changed little under perturbations of the input CC mask, whereas peripheral regions were more sensitive. The framework was further evaluated in group analyses of aging and Alzheimer's disease in datasets that differ in acquisition protocol and spatial resolution. Effect-size maps and quantitative comparisons showed that its results were largely consistent with TBSS and parcellation-based analyses in the overall spatial pattern of effects, while also revealing additional spatially localized differences. These findings suggest that the parameterization provides a simple, computationally efficient, and spatially detailed approach for investigating midsagittal CC microstructure, offering a structure-specific complement to whole-brain and region-based methods.

    Analysis choices can produce apparent rich-club differences: an Alzheimer's disease EEG case study

    arXiv:2611.19708v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: The weighted rich-club coefficient tests whether a network's most strongly connected nodes are preferentially interconnected. Its interpretation depends on two often-implicit choices that are easy to overlook: how the randomised comparison networks are constructed, and whether the hubs are re-identified in each comparison network or held fixed at those of the observed network. When hubs are defined by connection strength, these choices change what the coefficient actually tests. We examined how these choices affect inference using resting-state EEG from 20 people with Alzheimer's disease and 20 controls. Holding the data fixed, we compared four analysis pipelines, crossing two ways of building the comparison networks with the two hub choices, and ran a calibration study in which the same pipelines were applied to data constructed to contain no preferential hub coupling. An apparent eyes-open group difference in rich-club organisation appeared under only one of the four pipelines, the one combining a global weight reshuffle with re-identified hubs, and was absent under the other three. More tellingly, that same pipeline reported a group difference in 40% of cases where none was present, almost always higher in the Alzheimer's disease group, while the other three stayed near the expected 5%. This shows that the same data can support or dissolve an apparent rich-club difference depending on choices that are rarely reported. No single choice is universally correct, but reporting which were made, and why they fit the question being asked, is essential to interpreting the result.

    iEEG Can Help EEG: Improving Clinical Scalp EEG Analysis through Intracranial Priors

    arXiv:2611.20435v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: Scalp electroencephalography (EEG) is a cornerstone of clinical brain monitoring, making the quality of its signal fundamental to downstream tasks and models. However, that signal is heavily contaminated by non-neural interference and blurred by volume conduction. Existing methods operate almost exclusively within the scalp modality, so they inherit the contamination they would need to overcome. In practice, intracranial EEG (iEEG) observes the neural activity with far higher fidelity, yet it is invasive and therefore scarce, and the two modalities are rarely recorded as pairs at scale, share no common channel space, and offer no clean ground truth to directly supervise a mapping. This work provides evidence that a scarce clean modality can serve as a transferable prior for an abundant noisy one, offering a general recipe for cross-modal transfer among biosignals.     Analyzing Memory Effects in Large Language Models through the lens of Cognitive Psychology

    arXiv:2509.17138v4 Announce Type: replace Abstract: Memory, a fundamental component of human cognition, exhibits adaptive yet fallible characteristics as illustrated by Schacter's memory "sins".These cognitive phenomena have been studied extensively in psychology and neuroscience, but the extent to which artificial systems, specifically Large Language Models (LLMs), emulate these cognitive phenomena remains underexplored. This study uses human memory research as a lens for understanding LLMs and systematically investigates human memory effects in state-of-the-art LLMs using paradigms drawn from psychological research. We evaluate seven key memory phenomena, comparing human behavior to LLM performance. Both people and models remember less when overloaded with information (list length effect) and remember better with repeated exposure (list strength effect). These results reveal both alignments and divergences in how LLMs and humans reconstruct memory. The findings help clarify how memory-like behavior in LLMs echoes core features of human cognition, while also highlighting the architectural differences that lead to distinct patterns of error and success.

    arXiv:2608.00306v5 Announce Type: replace Abstract: Drug and disease perturbations often arise at molecular, synaptic or cellular scales, yet are often observed in population and whole-brain signals.
