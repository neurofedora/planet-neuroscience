    Ionic condition-dependent allostery shapes ribosomal dynamics

    by Danis Yangaliev, Gürol M. Süel, S. Banu Ozkan

    Ionic composition is a key determinant of ribosomal RNA structure and function. Metal cations such as potassium (K+) and magnesium (Mg2+) stabilize rRNA architecture. While certain positions in the ribosome are strictly occupied by either potassium or magnesium for distinct structural or functional reasons, their broader role in modulating ribosomal dynamics remains poorly understood. Here, we use microsecond-scale coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations of the bacterial 70S ribosome to examine how ionic composition affects structural flexibility and long-range communication. We compare a native ribosome with experimentally resolved ions to two variants: a magnesium-enriched model, in which K+ ions are replaced with Mg2+, and a hydrated magnesium model. While these substitutions do not aim to reproduce physiological ionic conditions, they provide a controlled framework to isolate the physical effects of ion identity on ribosomal dynamics. Increasing magnesium content reduces ionic and backbone mobility and enhances rigidity within the ribosomal core. Dynamic Flexibility Index analysis shows that these changes extend beyond local coordination sites, producing flexibility shifts at distances exceeding 30 Å. Dynamic Coupling Index analysis further reveals that ionic perturbations reshape long-range communication pathways. Functionally important regions, including the L1 and L7/12 stalks, exhibit distinct, ion-dependent responses, while the L10–L11 region shows enhanced long-range coupling under varying ionic conditions. These results demonstrate that ionic composition modulates both local stability and global ribosomal dynamics, with implications for translational efficiency and fidelity.

    A hybrid mixture approach for clustering and characterizing biomedical data

    by Kazeem A. Kareem, Fan Dai

    Model-based clustering (MBC) is widely used for identifying latent groups and characterizing heterogeneous structures in biomedical data. However, modern biomedical studies often generate datasets with large sample sizes and many variables, for which standard MBC approaches can be difficult to fit reliably and efficiently. Mixture of factor analyzers (MFA) addresses part of this challenge by representing high-dimensional covariance structures through a few underlying factors, thereby reducing the covariance dimensionality. However, current estimation methods for MFA are often computationally impractical for massive data due to the slow convergence of nested EM-type algorithms. Moreover, many existing implementations require a common number of latent factors across clusters, limiting their ability to capture group-specific structures. We propose a hybrid matrix-free computational scheme for accurate and efficient estimation of Gaussian mixture of factor analyzers, which avoids explicit construction or storage of large covariance matrices. We further develop a generalized version that allows different clusters to have varying numbers of factors, providing additional flexibility for group-specific characterization. Simulation studies show that the proposed approach achieves substantially faster convergence than the existing method, especially in high-dimensional settings, while preserving high clustering and estimation accuracy. Applications to the Wisconsin breast cancer dataset, a lymphoma gene-expression dataset, and a human brain single-cell RNA sequencing dataset demonstrate accurate identification of benign/malignant breast tumors, lymphoma subtypes, and neuronal and astrocytic cell populations. The fitted factor-loading structures reveal biologically meaningful differences in nuclear morphology for breast tumors, with malignant samples showing stronger irregularity-related variation, group-specific gene-expression heterogeneity among the three lymphoma subtypes, and distinct patterns of transcriptional variation between neurons and astrocytes, with neuronal variation reflecting a broader set of coordinated transcriptional directions and astrocytic variation concentrated in a more compact latent structure.

    Inferring population-level physiologically based model parameters for sleep in infancy and young childhood

    by Lachlan Webb, Andrew J. K. Phillips, James A. Roberts

    The sleep patterns of infants and young children differ from adult sleep patterns, with longer duration and multiple bouts per 24 hours. There is also considerable heterogeneity in infant sleep, both between individuals and within individuals across development. While mathematical models have been used to understand the mechanisms that regulate adult sleep, the development of sleep from infancy through early childhood has remained largely unexplored. Here we used an established mathematical model for adult sleep regulation to investigate how the underlying mechanisms of sleep mature from ages 1 month to 5 years, and identify a basis for inter-individual differences at each age. Using a Bayesian approach to estimate joint distributions of model parameters at different ages, we found that: (i) decreases in the rate of accumulation of sleep homeostatic pressure captured the reduction in sleep duration across development; while (ii) increases in the time scale of the sleep homeostatic drive captured the consolidation of sleep into fewer sleep bouts per day across development. In terms of inter-individual differences, we found larger spread in the parameters within the earlier stages of infancy (<1 year). The center and boundaries of the joint distributions evolved with age through the parameter space, converging towards previously described adult parameter values. A bifurcation analysis of the homeostatic time scale parameter revealed that progressive consolidation of sleep occurs through abrupt loss of bouts one at a time, punctuated by narrow intervals of non-entrained (>24-h) cycles. These results establish plausible, population-level trajectories in physiological parameters underpinning maturation of sleep regulation through infancy and early childhood.

    Rollout models of human planning with exact action probabilities

    by Jeroen Olieslagers, Wei Ji Ma

    One proposed mechanism for human planning is the mental simulation, or “rollout,” of potential action sequences to evaluate future outcomes. While rollout-based models have seen great success in artificial intelligence, cognitive models that describe how people use rollouts remain scarce, in part because they are difficult to fit to behavioral data. Existing methods rely on computationally intensive simulations that result in approximate, noisy estimates of latent planning parameters. We introduce ROSETTE (Rollouts with Stochastic Early Termination and Tractable Estimation), a rollout-based model for finite goal-directed tasks with absorbing goals that provides analytically tractable, simulation-free model fitting. ROSETTE treats planning as a sequence of mental simulations formalized as an absorbing Markov chain. The model deterministically accumulates analytically computed expected rollout evidence and performs a variable number of policy-update cycles. We fit ROSETTE to data from human participants in two multi-step planning tasks and found that it well accounted for a rich set of summary statistics of behavior. By making this specific class of absorbing-goal rollout models tractable, our framework provides a testable account of how people may simulate and evaluate possible futures.

    Simulation-guided design of exotendons to reduce the energetic cost of running

    by Jon Stingel, Nicholas Bianco, Carmichael Ong, Steve Collins, Scott Delp, Jennifer Hicks

    A passive device that attaches to the feet, called an exotendon, can reduce the energetic cost of running at moderate speeds, but its efficacy and optimal design parameters at higher speeds are unknown. Identifying optimal parameters at new speeds experimentally would require many experimental trials with different exotendon designs, which is challenging for participants at higher running speeds. We developed a muscle-driven simulation framework to predict the effect of various exotendon designs on the energetic cost of running at an experimentally untested speed (4 m/s). We used these predictions to select four designs, which we evaluated experimentally as users ran at this speed. The framework correctly predicted that an exotendon that reduced energetic cost at 2.7 m/s would also reduce energetic cost at 4 m/s (10% predicted vs. 5.7% measured) and that a short, stiff exotendon and a long, compliant exotendon would not significantly reduce energetic cost. However, exotendon parameters predicted by the simulation to maximize energetic savings did not significantly reduce energetic cost when evaluated experimentally. There was variability between participants in both the magnitude of maximum energy savings and the exotendon condition associated with those savings. In a 5-km time trial performed with and without the exotendon condition that elicited the largest energy savings for each participant during the experiment, we observed a lower average heart rate (-3.9 ± 3.8 beats/min; P = 0.03; mean ± standard deviation) and increased cadence (15.9 ± 9.6 steps/min; P = 0.002) when participants ran with the exotendon but did not observe a statistically significant difference in finishing time (-13.5 ± 24.6 sec; P = 0.3). These results demonstrate exotendons can reduce energetic cost across multiple running speeds and that predictive simulations provide a framework for guiding experiments to evaluate assistive device designs.

    Pupil dynamics reflect uncertainty-driven adjustments of probability learning

    by Aaron Greenhouse-Tucknott, Cedric Foucault, Anne Buot, Florent Meyniel

    Adaptive learning in dynamic environments depends on the brain’s ability to represent and respond to uncertainty. Changes in arousal-mediated pupil diameter provide insight into the neural basis of this process. Prior work has focused on magnitude-based inference tasks and revealed that learning is primarily influenced by the detection of change points and the corresponding modulation of arousal. In comparison, the role of arousal systems in learning contexts where changes are harder to detect, such as in probability learning, remains poorly understood. Here, we combined pupillometry with a probability learning task in which human participants estimated a hidden generative probability of stimuli that changed abruptly and unpredictably. A Bayesian ideal observer model was used to estimate trial-by-trial learning rates and two uncertainty-associated factors: change point probability (the probability that the generative process has abruptly changed) and prior uncertainty (about the current beliefs on this generative process). In line with normative theory, participants relied on prior uncertainty to adjust learning rates. Pupil analyses revealed temporally distinct representations of uncertainty, with phasic dilations tracking change point probability and tonic pupil dilations tracking prior uncertainty. Mediation analyses further indicated that both phasic and tonic pupil signals partially carried the effects of change point probability and prior uncertainty on learning, respectively. Critically, high tonic arousal weakened the coupling between phasic pupil responses and change point probability, accompanied by a reduced influence of change point probability on learning. Together, our results demonstrate that probability learning in a dynamic environment is underpinned by computationally rational integration of latent uncertainty factors and its contribution to belief updating,regulated through arousal-associated responses.
