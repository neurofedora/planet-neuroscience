    Mapping the Trade-Offs That Shape Mammalian Brain Network Architecture

    A brain's wiring must satisfy conflicting pressures, from the outside world (be capable) and from the inside (stay within finite resources). These pressures often have opposing requirements. Prior work studies these trade-offs mostly between 2-3 pressures at a time, and thus cannot say how many pressures shape brain wiring or for which trade-off brains decide. To see all possible trade-offs at once and to uncover the set of most important pressures, we generate 25,000 synthetic brain networks and describe each by 29 network properties. We then place 635 human (ages 6-22) and 224 mammalian (12 taxonomic orders) brain networks in this property space. The space is triangular, and follows three pressures: computational capability, robustness to damage, and metabolic efficiency. No network is optimal at all three, and biological brains cluster in one small niche of this triangle. There, they accept higher metabolic cost to gain both robustness and capability. Development and evolution move brains in the same direction; additionally, human maturation first raises capability, and later optimizes for efficiency. This study underscores the triangular trade-off between metabolic efficiency, computational capacity and robustness, and the narrow region brains occupy in this trade-off triangle, visible only from a multi-property, cross-species perspective.

    Domain-specific and domain-general signatures of dyscalculia in computational models of behavior and parietal cortex

    Developmental dyscalculia is a learning disorder characterized by marked difficulties in numerical cognition, yet its cognitive and neural underpinnings remain debated. Competing accounts attribute dyscalculia either to domain-specific imprecise representations of number or to domain-general deficits in the maintenance and integration of numerical information. Here, we test both accounts in 33 adolescents and young adults with dyscalculia and 33 age- and gender-matched controls (15-23 years) who performed a sequential numerosity comparison task during fMRI. Joint computational modeling of choices and response times allowed us to dissociate perceptual and memory noise from decision variables governing the speed-accuracy tradeoff, while complementary neuroimaging analyses characterized neural number representations and their network embedding. Specifically, we quantified the precision of parietal numerosity tuning with numerical population receptive field (nPRF) modeling and derived individualized functional network maps of the same region using community detection algorithms applied to connectivity patterns. Dyscalculic participants showed greater perceptual and memory noise but also a more cautious response criterion, trading speed for accuracy to compensate for representational imprecision. Neurally, the numerosity model explained less variance in parietal cortex, suggesting less precise numerical representations, and individualized network maps assigned less of this cortex to the dorsal attention network and more to a visual network. Rather than supporting a dichotomy between the two accounts, our findings suggest that dyscalculia reflects both less precise numerical representations in parietal cortex and an altered allocation of this cortex to distributed functional systems.

    Attention warps visual-semantic representations across the human cerebellum

    Humans effortlessly shift their attention between objects and events in the environment. These attentional shifts modulate the representation of information in many regions of the cerebral cortex, including those that represent visual-semantic information. Based on recent evidence that the cerebellum also represents semantic information, we hypothesized that attention would also shift semantic representations in the cerebellum. To test this hypothesis, we used fMRI to measure brain activity while participants watched naturalistic movie clips and were instructed to attend to either people or vehicles. We used a voxelwise encoding model framework to recover visual-semantic representations in the cerebral cortex and cerebellum under the two conditions, and quantified the direction and magnitude of visual-semantic tuning shifts between conditions. Attention to different semantic categories had a large effect on visual-semantic tuning in the cerebellum. Representations in some regions shifted toward the attended category, and representations in other regions shifted away from the attended category. Within cerebellar regions that best represent visual-semantic information, the tuning shifts were most pronounced in Crus I and II and in lobule VI. Further, the magnitude of attentional tuning shifts was greater in the cerebellum than in the cerebral cortex, particularly for tuning shifts towards the attended category. These differences suggest that attentional modulation in the cerebellum is different than in the cerebral cortex and may not solely be driven by cerebral inputs.

    Hemispheric Complementarity is Associated with Distinct Functional and Structural Brain Profiles

    Hemispheric specialization is thought to follow a principle of complementarity, with homotopic functions such as language and attention lateralizing to opposite hemispheres, yet its consistency across individuals and relevance for cognition remain unclear. Using multimodal neuroimaging and twin-family data from 987 Human Connectome Project participants, we identified three sustained attentional lateralization phenotypes (right, left, bilateral) that were independent of handedness, differed by sex, and tracked broad shifts in brain organization, with bilateral individuals showing stronger intrahemispheric and homotopic connectivity. Examining attention and language revealed three multidomain profiles (crowded, mixed, split) reflecting whether functions colocalize within or are distributed across hemispheres. Mixed organization was linked to higher planum polare myelination, whereas crowded organization showed higher fiber density connectivity across dorsal attention-default network. Both phenotypes were heritable, yet neither predicted cognitive performance. These findings reveal a brain organization in which specialization emerges from flexible, heritable patterns linking function, structural connectivity, and myeloarchitecture.

    EARLY-LIFE STRESS INDUCES SEXUALLY DIMORPHIC BRAIN AGING VIA MESOLIMBIC ALTERATIONS AND FRONTO-STRIATAL DYSFUNCTION

    Prenatal stress influences brain development; however, its long-term, region-specific effects on brain aging in males and females remain insufficiently characterized. This study examined the impact of unpredictable mild prenatal stress on behavior, cognition, and maternal care in C57BL/6J mice at middle (12 months) and old (18 months) ages. Stressed dams showed fragmented, unpredictable maternal behaviors, creating a stressful postnatal environment without impairing caregiving capacity. Both male and female offspring were born with lower birth weights and showed rapid postnatal growth, a trajectory associated with central insulin resistance and hippocampal signaling deficits. Sex-specific behavioral differences emerged with age. Middle-aged stressed males exhibited anhedonia, which progressed to hyperactivity, stereotypy, and persistent spatial memory deficits in old age. These outcomes corresponded with accelerated fronto-striatal decline, likely mediated by insulin resistance and increased rigidity of medial prefrontal cortex structures due to diminished local estrogenic activity. In contrast, middle-aged stressed females displayed hypoactivity and reduced anxiety, later developing anhedonia in old age while maintaining hippocampus-dependent memory. In females, this pattern supports a model in which a neuroestrogen - and growth factor-dependent feedback loop transiently preserves hippocampal plasticity but compromises dopaminergic function in reward circuits. This balance may deteriorate with reproductive senescence. Both sexes retained cognitive flexibility, indicating domain-specific rather than global cognitive impairment. These findings demonstrate that excessive prenatal glucocorticoid exposure and unpredictable postnatal care produce a combined hormonal and metabolic insult, resulting in sexually divergent trajectories of brain aging. The results underscore the necessity of incorporating sex as a biological variable in fetal programming research across the lifespan.

    Developmental prefrontal myelination shapes inhibitory interneuron function and adult social behavior

    Early-life adversity and abnormal myelination are implicated in psychiatric disorders, but how developmental myelination influences adult social cognition remains unclear. Here, we tested whether disrupting developmental myelination in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) impairs adult social behavior. Using complementary genetic strategies in male mice, we suppressed Myelin Regulatory Factor (Myrf)-dependent myelination locally in the juvenile mPFC or broadly in oligodendrocyte-lineage cells. Both approaches selectively impaired adult social novelty preference while sparing sociability, locomotion, and affective behaviors. Focal prefrontal dysmyelination also reduced social dominance. Spatial transcriptomics revealed coupling between myelin-related genes and parvalbumin (PV) interneuron markers in the healthy mPFC, which was disrupted by developmental hypomyelination. Consistent with this transcriptional alteration, PV interneurons showed reduced high-frequency firing despite preserved intrinsic excitability. In the human prefrontal cortex, MBP-PV expression was correlated in controls but not in individuals with schizophrenia. These findings indicate that developmental prefrontal myelination contributes to PV interneuron maturation and suggest that disrupted oligodendrocyte-interneuron coordination may contribute to impaired social recognition.

    Disrupting the Right Inferior Frontal Gyrus Increases Social Brain Activity During Emotion Recognition

    Neuroimaging studies have identified a specialised social brain network that supports emotion recognition and social cognition. This network performs a range of functions that include facial expression recognition and inferring the mental states of others. Studies of neuropsychological patients report that damage to the right inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), the top-down control area in this network, impairs facial expression recognition. To investigate how transient disruption of the right IFG affects the wider social brain we combined transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). Participants (n=20) were scanned across two sessions while performing a delayed match-to-sample emotion recognition task in which happy or fearful expressions were preceded by a congruent or incongruent word cue. Prior to each scan, thetaburst TMS was delivered over the right IFG or over the vertex control site (session order was balanced across participants). A group whole-brain analysis revealed that TMS to the IFG increased neural activity in multiple regions of the social brain network. These included the bilateral temporal pole, precuneus and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). This pattern of results shows that the disruption of top-down control mechanisms localised in the IFG alters the neural processing in remote nodes of the social brain network. Our findings are discussed within the wider context of how we recognise and understand the emotions of other people.

    rpSTS Stimulation Disrupts Shared Neural Responses to Social Content during Naturalistic Viewing

    Shared understanding of common experiences can foster feelings of social closeness and friendship, and help us understand those experiences. Recent work has suggested that patterns of neural activity shared across individuals, especially throughout the default mode network, may underlie shared representations. Here we used repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and functional magnetic resonance imaging during movie watching to show that shared neural patterns to common experiences rely on the encoding of social information from the environment by the right posterior superior temporal sulcus (rpSTS). An encoding model of movie features showed that rTMS selectively degraded social feature encoding in rpSTS, leaving low-level visual encoding in early visual cortex intact. Using natural language processing (NLP)-based analysis, we found that rpSTS stimulation was associated with reduced memory and intersubject pattern similarity across DMN and limbic networks during scenes central to the movie narrative. Projecting scene embeddings onto NLP-based concept axes revealed reductions in memory and neural pattern similarity for scenes conveying helplessness and empathy following rTMS. These findings provide causal evidence that the rpSTS is a key region that encodes shared social information into common neural representations, and suggest that disrupting its input leads DMN representations to become idiosyncratic.

    A human CHRNA6 3' UTR variant alters the molecular environment and microRNA engagement of the adolescent VTA without changing Chrna6 transcript abundance

    A single nucleotide polymorphism (rs2304297) in the 3' untranslated region (UTR) of CHRNA6, encoding the alpha6 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor subunit, is associated with adolescent smoking behavior. Humanized rats carrying the 'C' or 'G' allele show sex- and genotype-dependent responses to adolescent nicotine, yet the variant does not alter the protein sequence and the mechanism has remained unknown. We examined the molecular consequences of rs2304297 in vivo in the adolescent ventral tegmental area (VTA). Chrna6 transcript abundance did not differ by genotype, nor did transcript stability or polyadenylation site selection. The variant was instead associated with a transcriptional signature spanning myelin maintenance (Enpp6), lipid metabolism (Retsat), and genomic repair (Polq), and with elevated basal lipid peroxidation in alpha6GG animals. Computational folding predicted greater conformational flexibility of the 'G' allele, and spatial colocalization of Chrna6 with miRNA-365-5p was reduced in alpha6GG animals of both sexes. Coordinated expression of the two transcripts, however, followed a sex-by-genotype pattern, absent in alpha6CC females and alpha6GG males. These same groups showed elevated tyrosine hydroxylase expression and dopaminergic neuron number, and expansion of the Chrna6 co-expression network following nicotine. We propose that rs2304297 acts through two distinct layers: genotype determines spatial engagement of miRNA-365-5p with the transcript, while sex determines whether that engagement produces functional regulation. These findings identify a post-transcriptional mechanism for a non-coding nicotine addiction risk variant and demonstrate that its molecular effect can be uniform while its consequences are not.

    Ordinal position in auditory sequences is encoded in population dynamics in macaque frontal cortex

    The ability to extract structure from sensory sequences requires tracking not only stimulus identity but also ordinal position within a sequence. How and where ordinal information is represented in the primate brain remains unclear. Here, we recorded activity in populations of single neurons simultaneously from auditory cortex and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex in macaques passively listening to structured auditory sequences. Population-level analyses revealed that frontal cortex activity evolved progressively across the sequence, occupying distinct regions of neural state space for successive ordinal positions. In contrast, auditory cortex population activity reset with each element, producing stereotyped, stimulus-locked trajectories. Consistent with this dissociation, frontal cortex populations robustly decoded ordinal position independently of stimulus identity, whereas auditory cortex showed weak positional decoding despite strong identity representations. Frontal neurons further exhibited longer intrinsic timescales and reduced synchronization to element presentation, supporting integration of information across multiple elements. Together, these findings identify frontal activity as a key substrate for ordinal position encoding during auditory sequence processing, while auditory cortex primarily supports element-specific sensory representations.

    The anterior insular cortex mediates higher-order threat learning and memory

    Learning about potential threats is indispensable for survival, and its deficits underlie numerous neuropsychiatric disorders. Human brain imaging suggests the anterior insular cortex (AIC) as a key neural correlate of threat learning, yet its mechanistic role remains unclear. Leveraging multimodal systems neuroscience approaches, we demonstrate that AIC excitatory neurons, specifically layer 5 pyramidal neurons projecting to the basolateral amygdala, are essential for higher-order threat learning/memory. They are specifically required when a predictor is temporally separated from an aversive event or when the predictive information is embedded within a diffuse context. In contrast, the AIC is dispensable for elementary, temporally contiguous threat learning. Surprisingly, while the AIC promotes memory formation, it also constrains the level of fear expression during retrieval in a context-dependent manner. This bidirectional control is mediated by the recruitment of distinct AIC neuronal ensembles. Together, we identify a novel circuit mechanism for bidirectional regulation of higher-order threat learning/memory.

    The external parabrachial nucleus (elPBN) contributes to diverse behaviors, including pain and stress. Neurons in this region use glutamatergic and neuropeptidergic signaling, with pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP) expressed in a subset of glutamatergic neurons.     ATP1A3 E815K Disrupts Sodium Homeostasis and Excitability in Human Alternating Hemiplegia of Childhood Neurons

    Alternating hemiplegia of childhood (AHC) is a rare neurodevelopmental disorder primarily caused by heterozygous de novo mutations in the ATP1A3 gene, which encodes the neuron-specific catalytic 3 subunit of the P-type Na/K-ATPase. Among these, the E815K variant is the second most frequent ATP1A3 mutation in AHC and is associated with the most severe clinical phenotypes, characterized by frequent epileptic and dystonic episodes and profound developmental impairment, making it a key target for mechanistic study. Although E815K was shown to disrupt pump function in heterologous systems and animal models, its impact on human neurons remain poorly understood. To investigate its disease-relevant pathophysiology, we generated induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) from an AHC patient carrying the E815K mutation and differentiated them into mature neurons. Patient-derived neurons displayed normal differentiation and synaptogenesis, with preserved evoked synaptic transmission. However, 3-specific Na/K pump currents were nearly abolished, whereas 1 activity remained intact. This selective dysfunction was associated with a depolarized resting membrane potential, reduced input resistance, and diminished intrinsic excitability. Patient neurons generated fewer action potentials in response to depolarizing current steps, displayed altered firing patterns, and showed significantly decreased voltage-gated sodium current densities, while potassium conductance remained unaffected. Taken together, these results indicate that the ATP1A3 E815K mutation disrupts intracellular sodium homeostasis and consequently modifies the excitability of human neurons. Overall, these findings provide mechanistic insight into the molecular pathophysiology underlying this AHC variant and thereby establish a patient-derived neuronal model which serves as a platform for future drug screening approaches and therapeutic investigation.

    Spoken language comprehension relies on context-dependent neural weighting of syntax and prosody

    Comprehending spoken language requires extracting syntactic structure and meaning from a continuous acoustic signal. Psycholinguistic evidence points to a key role for prosody in this process. However, because interactions between prosody and syntax are typically demonstrated using carefully controlled sentence constructions, it remains unclear whether such interactions constitute a general, default property of spoken language processing. Addressing this question requires naturalistic, broad-coverage data. Here, we combined experimental psycholinguistics with naturalistic neuroscience to investigate the joint neural encoding of syntax and prosody in MEG recordings of 26 participants (19 female) listening to narrative speech. Linguistic annotations revealed that strong prosodic boundaries tend to coincide with syntactic phrase closures, and that the strength of prosodic boundaries scales with the number of phrases closed. Using time-resolved regression with temporal response functions, we modeled how these features relate to delta-band neural activity during continuous speech processing. Our results reveal a bidirectional interaction between syntax and prosody. Prosodic marking modulates the neural processing of syntax, with phrase closures being encoded more strongly when accompanied by strong prosodic boundaries. Conversely, syntactic structure shapes prosodic encoding, such that boundary strength is neurally encoded primarily at points of syntactic phrase closure. These findings demonstrate that syntax and prosody are encoded not independently, but in a context-dependent and mutually constraining manner, highlighting their joint role in guiding structure building during naturalistic spoken language comprehension.

    Human single-neuron correlates of somatosensation

    Understanding the neural correlates of consciousness poses a central challenge in neuroscience, with longstanding debate over which cortical areas underlie subjective sensory perception. We demonstrate that an orchestration of neural activity emerged across the grasp-related parietal-frontal network at near-threshold perception. Within this distributed network, we then focused on PPC and S1. While S1 responses closely tracked the amplitude of the physical stimulus, PPC activity covaried more strongly with awareness and confidence. This single-neuron evidence reveals PPC and S1 have complementary neural codes with PPC transforming physical stimulation into conscious perception whereas S1 is more sensitive to stimulus amplitude.
