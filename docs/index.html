    The Case for a Link Between Meissner's Corpuscle Density in the Digits and Hand Use: A Comparative Study in Nonhuman Primates

    Meissner's corpuscles (MCs) are abundant in the glabrous skin of primates, and important in fine dynamic touch and precision grip. Here, we compared MC density and size across the hands of strepsyrrhine, platyrrhine, and catarrhine nonhuman primate species, to determine any relationship between MC density and/or size and digit/hand function. Our findings support the hypothesis that there are distinct MC density patterns across the digits of different species that correspond to phylogeny and species-specific hand use.


    ABSTRACT

    Meissner's corpuscles (MCs) are abundant in the glabrous skin of most primates, and are specialized to detect mechanical stimuli important in fine dynamic touch and precision grip. While MCs are known to be concentrated in the distal regions of the hands in both humans and nonhuman primates (NHPs), past work indicates that their size and density can vary substantially. Multiple factors contribute to this, including the sample location, age, sex, health status of the animals used, and methodological differences in visualization and quantification. Here, we compared MC density and size across the hands of strepsyrrhine, platyrrhine, and catarrhine species, to determine any relationship between MC density and/or size and digit/hand function. Our findings reveal distinct MC density patterns across the digits of different species that corresponds to phylogeny and species-specific hand use. Galagos exhibit a linear trend with greatest MC density in the thumb. Owl and squirrel monkeys show a U-shaped pattern, with higher densities in digits 1 and 5, and macaque monkeys display a stepped pattern, with higher densities in digits 1 and 2 compared to the other fingers. These digit-specific distributions indicate a strong link between MC density and manual specialization, and provide additional insight into the evolution of tactile function and fine sensorimotor control in primates. We also compared mean MC diameters between species and found only partial support for the conclusions of earlier work suggesting that size increases with body mass.

    Student Entrepreneurial Behavior (SEB): A Bibliometric Analysis [version 3; peer review: 1 approved, 2 approved with reservations]

    Background Research on student entrepreneurship has concentrated largely on entrepreneurial intention, while Student Entrepreneurial Behavior (SEB), defined as the observable actions through which university students pursue entrepreneurial opportunities (opportunity identification, resource mobilization, venture creation, and business implementation), remains fragmented. Objective This study provides an exploratory bibliometric overview of SEB research, identifying publication trends, publisher distribution, thematic clusters, and research gaps within higher education. Methods Scopus records were retrieved through Publish or Perish (April 2025) using the keyword “Student Entrepreneurial Behavior”. After screening 42 refined records, 31 publications from 2020–2024 were included (28 journal articles and 3 conference papers). Co-occurrence mapping of title terms was performed in VOSviewer (full counting, minimum occurrence of two, association strength normalization, clustering resolution 1.00). Study designs were coded manually, separately from the bibliometric mapping. Results Annual output rose from 1 publication in 2020 to a peak of 11 in 2022, then fell to 5 in 2023 and 6 in 2024. Publishers based in the United Kingdom (n = 9) and Switzerland (n = 6) accounted for 15 of the 31 publications, reflecting publisher location, not research site. VOSviewer grouped 14 title terms into four clusters (students and education; behavior and intention; vocational contexts in China; influences on college students). Manual classification showed that 22 of 31 publications (71%) were cross-sectional, 3 (10%) longitudinal or repeated-measures, and 6 (19%) other. Conclusions SEB research is at an early, exploratory stage. The literature emphasizes intention-related antecedents and education, with few studies examining behavior over time. This is interpreted as a gap in research coverage rather than a measured intention–behavior gap. Future studies should apply longitudinal, mixed-method, and cross-cultural designs and use behavioral outcome indicators (e.g., venture-related activities and entrepreneurial experimentation) to inform entrepreneurship curricula. Findings are exploratory, limited by a small single-database corpus.

    Working Capital Management and Profitability of a Commercial Company Listed on the Lima Stock Exchange, 2013–2025 [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Background Working capital management is essential to financial performance because it involves controlling inventories, accounts payable, and accounts receivable. However, limited evidence exists on how sales growth may modify its relationship with profitability in Peruvian commercial companies. Objective To determine the relationship between working capital management and profitability in a commercial company listed on the Lima Stock Exchange during 2013–2025, assessing the moderating effect of sales growth. Methods An applied, quantitative, non-experimental, longitudinal, and explanatory study was conducted. The census sample consisted of 52 quarterly observations obtained from financial statements through documentary analysis. Descriptive statistics, Spearman correlation, and ordinary least squares regression models with interaction terms were applied. HAC/Newey-West robust standard errors were used at a 5% significance level. Results Working capital management, measured through the cash conversion cycle, showed a negative and significant relationship with ROA and net margin, whereas its relationship with operating profitability was not statistically significant. Sales growth did not significantly moderate the relationship with ROA, but showed a positive and significant moderating effect on net margin and operating profitability. Conclusions A longer cash conversion cycle was associated with lower ROA and net margin. However, the relationship between working capital management and profitability varied according to the profitability indicator and sales growth. Sales growth significantly moderated the relationship with net margin and operating profitability, but not with ROA.

    Household Water-Storage Practices and Drinking-Water Safety Awareness in Al-Kharj, Saudi Arabia [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Background Household water storage can introduce vulnerabilities into domestic drinking-water systems. This study assessed residents’ water sources, roof-tank maintenance practices, and water-safety perceptions in Al-Kharj, Saudi Arabia, alongside exploratory microbial testing in a linked subsample. Methods A cross-sectional survey was administered to 150 residents (response rate: 80.6%) between 15 July and 15 September 2025 using a pilot-tested 24-item questionnaire (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82). Thirty roof-tank water samples were linked to surveyed households for culture and PCR microbial analyses. Descriptive statistics and exploratory associations were evaluated. Results Participants ranged in age from 23 to 51 years (mean = 32.8, SD = 7.3). Primary drinking-water sources were bottled water (45.3%), well water (44.7%), and municipal supply (10.0%). Roof tanks were used by 96.0% of respondents; 48.7% reported not cleaning tanks, and 58.0% observed sediment. While 80.0% recognized that drinking water can transmit disease, only 66.0% believed roof-tank water could do so, and only 25.3% had attended water-related health education. In the linked subsample (n = 30), total coliforms were detected in 60.0% (18/30) by culture and 56.7% (17/30) by PCR (lacZ), while Escherichia coli was detected in 20.0% (6/30) by culture and 23.3% (7/30) by PCR. Widespread contamination was observed across maintenance categories, with small sample size precluding stable multivariable modeling. Conclusions Gaps exist between general water-safety awareness and specific household storage risk perceptions, alongside widespread roof-tank use and inconsistent cleaning. Strengthening community hygiene education and accessible maintenance guidance is critical to safeguard domestic water safety and support Sustainable Development Goals 3 and 6.

    Comparative assessment of the physical and mechanical properties of stem and branch wood of Samanea Saman in Ghana [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Background Branch wood is often discarded or used as fuelwood because its properties are insufficiently documented. This study compared stem and branch wood of Samanea saman, distinguishing heartwood from sapwood, to assess the potential of branch wood for structural and furniture applications. Methods Ten stem logs and ten branch logs were sampled from natural forest in Ghana. Moisture content and oven-dry density were determined using European Standard EN 13183-1 (2002) and the International Organization for Standardization method ISO 3131 (1975), respectively. Static bending, compression parallel to the grain and shear parallel to the grain were tested in accordance with British Standard BS 373:1957. Data were analysed using repeated-measures analysis of variance and least significant difference pairwise comparisons. Results Wood portion significantly affected all measured physical and mechanical properties. Moisture content ranged from 69.50% in stem heartwood to 85.50% in branch sapwood. Branch heartwood had the highest oven-dry density (819.10 kg/m3), while stem heartwood had the highest modulus of elasticity (12,214.19 MPa) and modulus of rupture (118.55 MPa). Branch heartwood recorded the highest compressive strength (60.60 MPa) and shear strength (18.93 MPa). Branch heartwood did not differ significantly from stem heartwood in modulus of rupture or compressive strength. Conclusions Stem heartwood showed the highest stiffness and bending strength. Branch heartwood demonstrated comparable performance to stem wood for selected strength properties and may be suitable for furniture components, joinery and moderately loaded applications. Branch sapwood is more appropriately considered for non-structural uses.

    Reconceptualizing Pluralism in Multicultural Societies: Insights from a Systematic Review of Empirical Studies [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Abstract Background Pluralism in multicultural societies is a crucial issue amid increasing global migration and identity complexity. Although many studies have discussed diversity, there has been no systematic study that maps the factors that shape pluralism, the relationships between factors, and the social models that support it. This void has an impact on the weak conceptual and practical foundation for formulating inclusive social cohesion strategies. Objective This review aims to identify the main factors that shape pluralism, analyze the relationships between concepts, and develop a conceptual model of pluralism in a multicultural society. Methodology The Systematic Literature Review approach was carried out following the PRISMA protocol and the quality assessment of MMAT 2018. Literature was searched through Scopus and Web of Science using the keywords pluralism, multiculturalism, identity, and policy. Inclusion criteria include empirical studies (2015–2024), English-speaking, and peer-reviewed. The data were analyzed thematically using NVivo 14. Results A total of 73 studies met the criteria and were analysed. Ten main factors were found, including: multicultural education, social awareness, local wisdom, cross-cultural interaction, media, state policies, religion, national identity, globalization, and inclusive leadership. The relationship between factors is systemic, with social awareness and tolerance as the transition nodes towards pluralism. The conceptual model shows that pluralism is formed from the simultaneous interaction of education, policy, media, and cultural values. These findings integrate critical education theory, interculturalism, and social justice. Conclusion Pluralism is a product of multidimensional interaction that requires a cross-sectoral integrative approach. This study recommends evidence-based policy, education, and media representation strategies to strengthen sustainable pluralism.

    A single inference process underlies the behavior and neural circuits of working memory

    arXiv:2611.22054v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Working memory holds past inputs online for future behavior. Despite decades of research, accounts of working memory remain divided across levels of analysis, with long-standing debates on whether its capacity reflects discrete slots or continuous resources, how attention improves recall, and whether its representations rely on persistent neural firing or activity-silent states. Here we show that MemGP, a theory that is specified at the computational, algorithmic, and circuit levels of analysis simultaneously, can explain diverse behavioral and neural phenomena in working memory. At the computational level, MemGP casts working memory as probabilistic inference over a continuous function on feature space, with a variational objective that balances memory strength against a Gaussian-process prior. At the algorithmic level, writing, storing, and reading of memories correspond to approximating this posterior with a finite set of inducing points, the resource that all items share. In simulations, MemGP reproduces load-dependent precision loss, attentional benefits during maintenance, and inter-item attraction and repulsion, in each case revealing multiple complex computational dependencies. At the circuit level, the algorithm analytically (without training) maps onto a recurrent circuit, including its architecture and dynamics. Beyond reproducing neural phenomena such as delay-dependent drift, this analytical map deduces center--surround connectivity, a property long assumed in attractor models, and a structured combination of persistent activity and synaptic weights to hold memories. Thus, a single inference objective not only jointly predicts the behavioral and neural signatures of working memory, but also addresses, across several instances and different levels of analysis, existing competing explanations.

    Earth-strength magnetic-field axis modulates non-spatial probabilistic decision-making in humans

    arXiv:2611.22386v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Magnetoreception has been primarily studied in orientation and navigation, but whether Earth-strength magnetic fields influence non-spatial decisions remains unclear. Evidence for human magnetic responses is limited, and the transduction mechanism is unknown. Using a Go stone selection paradigm in which near-zero geomagnetic field and Larmor-frequency conditions previously showed lower black stone rates, we tested whether non-spatial choice depends on magnetic-field geometry. Fasted men played in pairs under ten static two-seat configurations varied component-wise at near-constant north-seat intensity. The north-seat black stone rate was lower under the two configurations with horizontal fields transverse to the geomagnetic field than under the other eight, which did not differ detectably. The difference emerged promptly, without evidence of outcome-driven learning or configuration-dependent associations with the partner's preceding actions, and process analyses linked it to the joint parity-choice outcome without isolating either step. A Larmor-frequency difference observed over the ambient field was not detected over horizontal-only or vertical-only backgrounds, suggesting a potential magnetoresponsive axis, although the background difference was statistically unresolved. Frequency, light-spectrum, and posture analyses support a radical-pair mechanism as a leading working hypothesis. These findings associate the geomagnetic field axis with human non-spatial choice, providing a paradigm linking magnetic sensing to decision processes.

    The shape of individual differences in judgment: shared axes, personal weights, and when their spread crosses the consensus

    arXiv:2611.22440v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: People differ in how they judge faces, artworks, photographs and jokes. Previous work measured the size of these differences; we ask what shape they take. We wrote three candidate shapes as nested generative models (same-sign reweighting of shared dimensions, a linear transform that allows reversal, and stimulus-specific offsets) and compared how well they predict people unseen in training, using ten public datasets (about 20,000 raters). The models recovered a person's judgment space from 10-20 judgments and predicted judgments of unseen stimuli. For faces, reweighting was sufficient, and weights opposing the population were not statistically detected.     arXiv:2611.22639v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Central pattern generators (CPGs) provide a biologically inspired foundation for adaptive locomotor control, yet systematically characterizing their dynamic regimes in spiking neural networks remains challenging.     Fully Spiking Neural Implementation of Lambda-Calculus Beta-Reduction via Biologically Documented Mechanisms

    arXiv:2611.23182v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: We present the first complete implementation of $\beta$-reduction in the untyped $\lambda$-calculus using exclusively spiking neural dynamics. Every step of the reduction---redex detection, subterm segmentation, variable-binding detection, De~Bruijn index arithmetic, token copying, and inter-step role maintenance---is performed by leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons with explicit membrane dynamics, exponential synaptic currents, axonal delays, and pair-based spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP). All synaptic weights are derived analytically from measured EPSP amplitudes; no hand tuning is performed. To our knowledge this is the first Turing-complete symbolic computation performed entirely within spike dynamics using biologically grounded circuit primitives. We discuss implications for neuromorphic proof assistants and the neural basis of symbolic reasoning.

    Why do continual physical learners need sleep?

    arXiv:2611.22070v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: Sleep is widespread across the animal kingdom, including in organisms without a centralized nervous system. Does learning create a need for sleep, even without biology? To explore this question, we study a disordered spring network trained to perform a sequence of mechanical allosteric tasks through local changes in bond stiffness. We find that continual learning progressively reduces the network's ability to acquire new tasks as individual bonds become increasingly specialized. We identify an exact algebraic relationship between the polarization of bond dominance and the network's remaining learning capacity, providing a quantitative measure of this loss of adaptability. We then demonstrate that random internal perturbations allow individual bonds to estimate their mechanical dominance and locally adjust their stiffnesses to restore learning capacity. However, the forces associated with active learning bias these local measurements and interfere with restoration. Separating learning and repair into wake-like and sleep-like phases removes this interference and improves continual-learning performance. Our results suggest that the need for offline restorative phases can emerge from the mechanics of physical learning, even in systems without biological components.
