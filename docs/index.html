    Speaking up at work: When support meets safety [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Background Speaking up at work can contribute to organizational improvement, but expressing ideas and concerns may involve interpersonal risks. Supportive coworkers and receptive managers represent important social experiences associated with employee voice, while perceived interpersonal safety may shape their motivational significance. This study examines the relationships of coworker support and managerial openness with voice behavior through autonomous motivation to voice, considering psychological safety as a boundary condition. Methods A cross-sectional online survey was conducted among 350 employees of a second-tier subsidiary within an Indonesian state-owned enterprise group. Data were analyzed using partial least squares structural equation modeling to examine direct, indirect, moderating, and conditional indirect relationships. Results Autonomous motivation to voice was positively associated with voice behavior. Coworker support and managerial openness also showed positive direct associations with voice behavior, but neither had a significant relationship with autonomous motivation to voice at the reference level of psychological safety. Psychological safety strengthened the relationship between coworker support and autonomous motivation to voice and its indirect association with voice behavior. However, comparable moderating relationships involving managerial openness were not supported. Conclusions The findings distinguish supportive social experiences associated with speaking up from those associated with employees’ autonomous reasons for doing so. Psychological safety emerges as a boundary condition linking coworker support with autonomous motivation to voice, highlighting the importance of interpersonal safety in understanding the motivational foundations of voice. These findings suggest that organizational efforts to encourage voice should consider both supportive workplace relationships and employees’ personal reasons for speaking up.

    Sustainable Low-Carbon Development through Renewable Energy, Green Buildings, and Climate Finance: A Systematic Literature Review and Future Research Agenda [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Background Sustainable low-carbon development is increasingly central to global climate mitigation, carbon neutrality, and net-zero transition strategies. Renewable energy, green buildings, and climate finance are essential components of this transformation. However, existing research remains fragmented, with limited systematic integration of these interrelated domains. This study aims to examine the evolution, intellectual structure, and thematic development of sustainable low-carbon research and identify emerging knowledge gaps and future research priorities. Methods A systematic literature review was conducted using the Scopus database, covering peer-reviewed journal articles published between 2004 and 2024. Following PRISMA-guided study selection procedures, 970 articles were included in the final review. Bibliometric analysis was performed using Bibliometrix (Biblioshiny) and VOSviewer to evaluate publication trends, disciplinary contributions, research productivity, keyword networks, and thematic evolution. Qualitative thematic analysis was employed to synthesize major research streams and identify future research directions. Results The findings indicate substantial growth in sustainable low-carbon development research, particularly after 2015. Energy and Environmental Science emerged as the leading subject areas, while sustainability, circular economy, climate change, renewable energy, carbon neutrality, and green hydrogen were prominent research themes. Four interconnected thematic streams were identified: (1) renewable energy transition and decarbonization, (2) green building technologies and sustainable construction, (3) climate finance and sustainable investment, and (4) integrated sustainable low-carbon development pathways. Emerging research priorities include green hydrogen, energy storage, artificial intelligence, digital sustainability technologies, climate finance effectiveness, net-zero buildings, and integrated decarbonization frameworks. Conclusions This review integrates technological, financial, and environmental perspectives to advance understanding of sustainable low-carbon development. The findings provide a foundation for future interdisciplinary research and offer policy-relevant insights for accelerating renewable energy adoption, sustainable infrastructure investment, climate finance effectiveness, and global net-zero transitions.

Methods Translation of WDL workflows into Nextflow ones allows usage of both languages using the same execution engine.     Brain-Heart Interactions Underlying Differential Recovery after Transient Systemic Anoxia

    Global cerebral anoxia is a leading cause of death, and resuscitated patients often remain persistently affected by neurological deficits. While previous studies suggest that brain–heart electrophysiological interactions may predict severity and prognosis after hypoxic brain injury coma, little is known about the brain–heart dynamics at near death. Gaining insight into these mechanisms is crucial for developing targeted interventions in critical conditions. Using a rodent model of reversible systemic anoxia (n = 29, male and female rats), we investigated whether brain–heart interactions during the asphyxia onset could predict the return of brain electrical activities after resuscitation. Electrophysiological recordings confirmed that cerebral activity declines following asphyxia, coinciding with increased heart rate variability. Notably, the strong coupling between cardiac parasympathetic activity and high-frequency brain activity in the somatosensory cortex and hippocampus serves as a key predictor of survival. Our study underscores the potential involvement of the brain–heart axis in the physiology of dying and the potential prognostic significance of the underlying mechanisms, paving the way for translational research into critical care, based on new characterizations of cardiac reflexes and brain–heart interactions.

    Large-Scale Neural Slowing Measured with Electroencephalography Indexes Secondary Thalamic Degeneration in Stroke

    A central priority in stroke recovery research is the development of useful biomarkers that reveal underlying disease states to aid diagnosis, prognosis, stratification, and treatment, ideally using tools that are readily available in clinical settings. Large-scale neural slowing has the potential to be such a biomarker, but its usefulness is currently limited by uncertainty about its nature and relation to underlying pathophysiology. In this work, we sought to address these gaps by parameterizing abnormal resting-state electroencephalography spectral features across the scalp and investigating their relationship to thalamic atrophy and dysfunction, using structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and a computational model of corticothalamic circuit dynamics, respectively. As predicted, stroke patients (n = 25; 15 males, 10 females) exhibited widespread spectral abnormalities, including significantly increased aperiodic exponent and offset, lower alpha frequency, and reduced beta power, which together can account for the frequently observed shift in power toward low frequencies after stroke. Furthermore, corticothalamic models fit to power spectral densities across the scalp inferred broad thalamic disinhibition. Crucially, these abnormalities (except beta reductions) were strongly associated with ipsilesional thalamic atrophy measured with MRI, despite the lack of direct thalamus damage in this sample. Overall, this work provides a deeper characterization of large-scale electrophysiological abnormalities after stroke and reinforces their potential as clinically feasible biomarkers of poststroke pathophysiology.

    Self-Reported Uterine Fibroids and Cardiometabolic Risk Among Non-Hispanic Black Women Aged 20–54 Years: A Nationally Representative Cross-Sectional Analysis of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 1999–2006 [version 1; peer review: awaiti

    Background Uterine fibroids disproportionately affect non-Hispanic Black women, who also bear a disproportionate burden of cardiometabolic disease. Evidence on the association between fibroids and cardiometabolic risk factors remains inconsistent. We examined the association between self-reported uterine fibroids and prevalent hypertension, diabetes, and obesity among non-Hispanic Black women aged 20–54 years. Methods Cross-sectional analysis of pooled NHANES 1999–2006 data (four cycles). Hysterectomy status was reconstructed using cycle-specific NHANES skip logic to preserve the full eligible sample. Survey-weighted modified Poisson regression estimated prevalence ratios (PR) with 95% CIs, adjusting for age, BMI, smoking, poverty-income ratio, and menopausal status. Missing covariates were handled with multiple imputation (20 datasets). Sensitivity analyses used alternative hypertension (≥140/90 mmHg) and obesity (BMI ≥25 and ≥ 35 kg/m2) definitions, and included women with prior hysterectomy. Results Among 987 women (weighted n = 6,864,326), 16.3% self-reported fibroids. Women with fibroids were older (41.2 vs 33.9 years), had higher BMI (32.6 vs 31.0 kg/m2), and higher prevalence of hypertension (50.4% vs 32.2%) and diabetes (10.7% vs 5.3%). In fully adjusted models, fibroids were not significantly associated with hypertension (PR 1.13; 95% CI 0.95–1.34), diabetes (PR 1.42; 95% CI 0.87–2.30), or obesity (PR 1.08; 95% CI 0.90–1.31). Using the 140/90 mmHg threshold (PR 1.36; 95% CI 1.07–1.73) and in the hysterectomy-inclusive analysis (PR 1.16; 95% CI 1.03–1.31), statistically significant associations with hypertension were observed. Conclusions After age adjustment, self-reported fibroids were not independently associated with hypertension, diabetes, or obesity in this sample. The significant hypertension findings at the 140/90 mmHg threshold and in the hysterectomy-inclusive analysis may reflect prevalence-ratio compression at the 130/80 threshold rather than a genuine absence of association. Renewal of NHANES uterine fibroid data collection in contemporary cycles is warranted.

    Negotiating culture and inclusion in village environmental policymaking in Tuwed Bali, Indonesia [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Background Culturally grounded environmental policy must negotiate customary authority, administrative responsibilities and unequal opportunities to participate. This study examines how these concerns were articulated in the development of an inclusive village environmental regulation in Tuwed, Bali, Indonesia. Methods An interpretive documentary case study analysed nine supplied files comprising three regulation drafts, a critical review, minutes of a focus group discussion, a village profile and three government presentations. The minutes and their appended draft were treated as one linked source. Structured extraction, comparison of policy provisions and thematic interpretation traced relationships between stakeholder statements and institutional design. Recorded statements were translated from Indonesian; they were not treated as independently collected interviews. Results Five themes linked the documentary evidence: negotiating village jurisdiction; reconciling customary practice with conservation; translating inclusion into accessible procedures; organising deliberation and accountability; and specifying resources and evaluation. The dated draft explicitly addressed accessible communication, representation of persons with disabilities and women, customary institutions, village monitoring, funding and a three-year review. However, debate over monitor independence and a customary-ceremony exception for protected wildlife remained unresolved. The village profile also contained inconsistent population totals, and a presentation named a regulation whose signed promulgated text was unavailable. Conclusions The case supports an interpretive model of negotiated cultural inclusion in environmental policy design. Cultural recognition becomes operational through accessible procedures, bounded authority and accountable deliberation. Draft provisions and recorded intentions do not establish implementation, legal validity or environmental effectiveness. Verification of regulatory status and prospective evaluation are necessary before stronger claims can be made.

    Patient and Clinician Perspectives on Socio-Ecological Determinants of Rheumatoid Arthritis: A Comparative Qualitative Study [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Objective Rheumatology care increasingly recognizes that rheumatoid arthritis (RA) risk, symptom activity, and disease management are shaped by social and environmental context; however, practical frameworks for integrating this information into routine clinical care remain limited. We aimed to identify actionable socio-ecological factors associated with RA onset and activity, and to examine barriers to translating exposure-related information into routine clinical care. Methods We conducted a cross-case comparative qualitative analysis of semi-structured interviews with patients with RA and rheumatology clinicians. Five patients were selected using stratified random sampling across U.S. climate regions defined by the National Centers for Environmental Information, and five clinicians were recruited through convenience sampling. Interviews explored perceived social and environmental influences on RA symptoms and management, as well as the feasibility of integrating exposure-related insights into clinical workflows. Transcripts and detailed interview notes were analyzed iteratively using inductive and deductive coding with cross-case comparisons guided by the socio-ecological model. Results Participants included five patients (80% female; 80% White, non-Hispanic; ages 47–68) and five clinicians (four rheumatologists, one pharmacist). Patients reported that environmental and lifestyle factors were rarely addressed in their care and emphasized emotional burden, uncertainty, and a desire for actionable self-management strategies. Clinicians focused on structural determinants, comorbidities, and population-level risk stratification. Both groups acknowledged multilevel influences, including stress, insurance barriers, occupational exposures, community stressors, and climate-related effects. The greatest alignment occurred at the ecosystem level, where patients emphasized lived experience and adaptation while clinicians emphasized mechanisms and epidemiologic patterns. Conclusions Patients and clinicians share recognition of multilevel influences on RA but differ in framing and application. These findings highlight opportunities to integrate socio-ecological context into RA care through structured, low-burden approaches that support shared sense-making without increasing clinical workload.

    Policy Brief on Organisational Structure, Performance Monitoring, and Academic Staff Performance in Selected Private Chartered Universities in Ankole Sub-Region, Uganda [version 3; peer review: 2 approved with reservations]

    Background Academic staff performance is fundamental to teaching quality, research productivity, institutional effectiveness, and the competitiveness of higher education institutions. However, private chartered universities in Uganda continue to experience challenges associated with rigid organisational structures, inconsistent performance monitoring, and limited developmental feedback. This policy brief presents evidence from a convergent parallel mixed-methods study conducted in selected private chartered universities in Ankole Sub-Region, Uganda, to inform institutional and national policy reforms. Policy and Implications Quantitative data were collected from 330 academic staff through stratified sampling, while qualitative data were obtained from 10 Deans using purposive sampling. The findings showed that participating universities predominantly operated under mechanistic organisational structures characterised by hierarchical authority, centralised decision-making, and rigid formalisation (M = 3.94, SD = 0.90). Performance monitoring practices were moderately implemented (M = 3.91, SD = 1.03). Organisational structure was positively associated with academic staff performance (r = 0.65, p < .05), while performance monitoring demonstrated a slightly stronger relationship (r = 0.66, p < .05). Qualitative evidence further indicated that performance monitoring was often irregular, compliance-oriented, and rarely supported by structured developmental feedback. Recommendations Private chartered universities should adopt more participatory and decentralised governance structures, institutionalise standardised and developmental performance monitoring frameworks, strengthen continuous professional development, and invest in digital performance management systems to improve accountability and academic staff performance. Conclusions The findings indicate that improving organisational structures and strengthening performance monitoring systems can enhance academic staff performance. Implementing evidence-informed governance and performance management reforms may contribute to improved teaching quality, research productivity, and institutional effectiveness in Uganda’s private chartered universities.

    Beyond profiles: Supervised repeat annotation using protein embeddings

    by Kaiyu Qiu, Jan Ludwiczak, Andrei N. Lupas, Stanislaw Dunin-Horkawicz

    Protein repeats give rise to diverse protein structures and functions, yet their sequence-based detection is often challenging because individual repeat units can diverge substantially in sequence while retaining similar structures. Most sensitive approaches rely on sequence information directly represented by alignments or derived profiles, thereby limiting flexibility and scalability. Here, we introduce TREAD (Transfer learning-based REpeat Annotation using Protein EmbeDdings), a supervised framework that reformulates repeat detection as a residue-level annotation problem and operates directly on embeddings from protein language models. Instead of constructing explicit probabilistic profiles, TREAD learns repeat-specific features implicitly, enabling residue-resolved scoring and flexible repeat segment extraction. Across complementary benchmarks based on RepeatsDB and Pfam, TREAD consistently matches or outperforms the widely used profile-based tool HMMER, particularly in low-data and high-divergence settings. The model exhibits robustness to score thresholding and demonstrates better generalization to independent test sets, including those containing remotely related families. To illustrate the practical utility of TREAD, we applied it to survey β-propeller proteins across the AlphaFold Database and representative proteomes to generate a comprehensive census of this fold. This analysis highlights extensive propeller diversity, identifies lineage-specific expansion patterns across the tree of life, and suggests previously unrecognized relationships between propellers and other repeat folds. Together, TREAD provides a flexible and scalable alternative to profile-based repeat annotation and establishes a general motif-centric framework for protein sequence annotation.

    Vascular modelling of peripheral ischaemic disease response

    by Lachlan Arthur, Michele Carrabba, Paolo Madeddu, Steven McDougall, Vassilis Sboros

    Ischaemic disease presents a significant challenge in medical science due to its widespread manifestations in the body and its tendency to only become overt in its late stages, beyond where current intervention methods are effective. Diseases such as coronary heart disease and peripheral artery disease (PAD) represent leading causes of death and are detrimental to quality of life. Focussing on the latter, PAD incurs an amputation rate of around 25%, largely owing to microvascular disease that prevents tissue recovery even after revascularisation. Current methods for investigating PAD either cannot chart specific vascular determinants, such as with in vivo imaging, or deform and remove subjects from longitudinal studies, such as with ex vivo tissue sampling. Presented to aid live research, computational modelling can collate information and build models of ischaemia using available information and provide a reasonable approximation of the disease’s chronology and thus support understanding and advance research of PAD. Proposed here, is a 3D in silico model of ischaemic disease in the limb using a system of purpose-built platforms. Starting with a real micro-computed tomography image of the murine hindlimb, a realistic vessel bed was generated using vascular graphing software, ViGiL. From here, the model was realised in a defined pressure gradient via flow-modelling software, numPTI. With the base model of lower limb arteries, collateral vasculature was generated throughout the vessel space. This provides a bypass system for adaptation post-ischaemia. Upon ischaemic induction, the collateral bed responds to the loss of flow and successfully adapted to recover flow with similar density and distribution of diameters in collateral arterioles (p>> 0.05). Moreover, a satisfactory flow recovery of 50% was achieved after 6 days, showing that the model can reflect the specific vascular environment in which disease is incited. The proceeds of this study lay the groundwork for truly realistic ischaemic models including moving towards a predictive model than can signify when and where key indicators of PAD manifest.

    CoEVFold suite: User friendly pipelines to visually represent protein coevolution

    by Chris L. B. Graham, Liam Cremona, Robin Little, Christopher D. A. Rodrigues

    Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) data underlies current principles in protein folding and protein-protein interaction prediction, from which deep-learning models trained on protein sequence and structure datasets can predict protein structure. However, what is missing are user-friendly tools that enable researchers to identify and visualise coevolution, the principal signal that these MSAs encode. Here we present tools to identify and visualize coevolution, through a pipeline (CoEVFold) that uses basic direct coupling algorithms derived from GREMLIN and an input alignment of sequences from MMseqs2. We also provide notebooks to calculate coevolution with alternative methods such as CCMpred or plmc. The pipeline generates a visual representation of coevolution for a single protein but can also represent coevolution of homomeric or heteromeric protein complexes, as well as coevolution within protein networks. The input for this pipeline can be an amino acid sequence, or protein structures from AlphaFold/RoseTTAFold, the user’s own files or the PDB. Using proteins from well-studied prokaryotic and eukaryotic model systems (Bacillus subtilis, Escherichia coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae), as well as phage proteins, we show that CoEVFold detects coevolution between proteins known to interact, between protomers of known oligomers, and within known protein complexes. Benchmarked against 18 heteromeric complexes of known structure, the top-ranked inter-protein couplings identified interface contacts with a precision of 0.8 or more in 7 complexes, and CoEVMapper rankings agreed with STRING associations for an Escherichia coli cell-wall synthesis network (ROC AUC 0.72–0.74). For homo-oligomers, the top-ranked coevolution pairs not explained by the monomer structure were protomer–protomer contacts in 25% (SpoIIIE) and 47% (MlaD) of cases, against 2% or less expected by chance. The coupling calculation for a ~ 950-residue complex took 114 s with GREMLIN, compared with 116 s for CCMpred and 2,060 s for plmc. Collectively, this suite of tools, named ‘CoEVFold suite’, has broad applicability and provide a useful toolkit to those interested in dissecting protein-protein interactions and networks.

    Deciphering the replication-division coordination in E. coli: A unified mathematical framework for systematic model comparison

    by Alexandre Perrin, Marie Doumic, Meriem El Karoui, Sylvie Méléard

    Despite extensive research, the quantitative principles that govern the coordination between DNA replication and cell division in bacteria remain debated. Multiple theoretical models have been proposed, some postulating that a single regulatory process is sufficient to ensure replication–division coordination, while others argue that two concurrent processes are required for robust control. In this work, we develop a unifying mathematical framework within which models can be consistently formulated, qualitatively analysed and quantitatively compared. This framework also allows us to propose a new double-process model. Through theoretical analysis, we establish the necessary and sufficient conditions under which single-process models can reproduce physiological cell behaviours. Beyond the correlation-based analyses extensively used to date, we further demonstrate within a comprehensive statistical framework that double-process models more accurately recapitulate experimental data across all growth conditions. Specifically, the new model we propose robustly captures the replication-division coordination in every growth regime, thereby providing a foundation for future mechanistic studies.

    Differential vulnerability of stimulus-locked and persistent gamma oscillations: Implications in schizophrenia

    by Daniel W. Chung, G. Bard Ermentrout

    Working memory depends on gamma oscillations generated across sensory and prefrontal cortices. In primary visual cortex (V1), stimulus-locked gamma oscillations encode stimulus information, while in prefrontal cortex (PFC), persistent gamma oscillations maintain this information after the stimulus is removed. In schizophrenia (SZ), gamma power is reduced in both V1 and PFC, consistent with deficits in sensory encoding and working memory maintenance. Both oscillation regimes arise from a canonical microcircuit involving pyramidal neurons (PNs) and parvalbumin-expressing interneurons (PVIs). Yet, whether the two regimes are similarly or differentially vulnerable to synaptic alterations within this circuit in SZ remains unknown. To investigate this question, we used a mean-field model of the PN-PVI circuit, in which the two regimes were distinguished by NMDA conductance values reflecting the difference in GluN2B expression between human PFC and V1. We then assessed three synaptic alterations quantified using molecular and structural proxies in postmortem SZ brains: lower excitatory inputs to PVIs (E→I), lower inhibitory inputs to PNs (I→E), and greater variability in E→I strength. Each alteration produced larger gamma power deficits in the persistent regime than in the stimulus-locked regime. Applied together, these alterations interacted synergistically to reduce gamma power, with the persistent regime exhibiting a more pronounced deficit. Bifurcation analyses revealed that this differential vulnerability arises from a wider margin of oscillatory stability in the stimulus-locked regime, and that reduced I→E strength additionally abolished the bistability required for the persistent regime. These findings predict that a larger magnitude of synaptic alteration is required to disrupt gamma oscillations in V1 than in PFC, consistent with postmortem reports of more severe alterations in V1 in SZ. Together, they establish a differential vulnerability model of cortical gamma oscillations in SZ, in which regional differences in oscillatory stability determine how much synaptic alteration is required to disrupt oscillations with distinct dynamics.

    TRAFIKK: Systematic prediction and mechanistic interpretation of anticancer drug synergies

    by Marco Fariñas, Viviam Bermúdez, Eirini Tsirvouli, Kristine Lippestad, John Zobolas, Tero Aittokallio, Kaisa Lehti, Åsmund Flobak

    Effective drug combination therapies can improve cancer treatment, yet the mechanistic basis of drug synergy remains poorly understood. Most computational approaches prioritise predictive accuracy over molecular mechanistic interpretability, providing limited insights into how synergistic effects emerge across signalling contexts. We developed Trafikk, a molecular-signalling network-based framework that simulates drug perturbations in cell-line-specific computational models to mirror functional outcomes in experimental combination screens. Across two independent large-scale datasets, Trafikk identified synergistic combinations with >77% recall. Functional response predictions revealed both conserved and context-dependent mechanisms. While AKT-MEK co-inhibition consistently disrupted coordinated survival and apoptotic signalling in 742 cell lines, PI3K-BCL2 synergy arose through distinct death programs shaped by cell-context-specific network constraints. Trafikk combines predictive performance with mechanistic interpretability, capturing how and why drug synergy emerges across cellular contexts. Source code, installation instructions and usage tutorial are freely available at https://github.com/druglogics/trafikk.

    When Can an AI Agent Serve as a Model of Human Psychopathology?

    arXiv:2611.28405v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Language models and AI agents are increasingly described in psychiatric terms, such as anxious, depressed, addicted or helpless. A recent study even proposed language models as experimental systems for psychiatry and as a platform for piloting psychological treatments. We ask when an experiment on an AI agent can provide evidence about a specified human psychiatric process. A claim to such evidence has three parts. Something changed in the agent, and the agent's history caused the change. Nothing other than a process shared with people explains that change. The change matches one theory of the human process and not that theory's rivals. Psychiatry and its neighbouring fields have an evidence standard for each part, from the diagnostic manuals' rule for induced states, from clinical test scores and from animal models. We translate each standard into a criterion that a machine study can meet. For a language model the most critical alternative to a shared process is extrapolation from its training text, which already contains descriptions of how people respond to trauma and how they answer questionnaires. We show how to probe that alternative. Further, we audited forty-three published studies that apply psychiatric terms to a machine or induce a comparable state in one. Most compared the machine with an identical copy that had not gone through the history that induced the state. None excluded extrapolation from training text. Three tested a psychiatric theory against its rivals, and none of the three tests was conclusive. Until better evidence exists, a treatment pilot on a language model is best read as the model's forecast of patient response, and not as an experiment on a model organism.
